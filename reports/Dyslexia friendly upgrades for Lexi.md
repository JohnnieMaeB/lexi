# Let Spacing, Speech and Safety Steer Lexi

The upgrades with the best evidence for Lexi are **adjustable letter and word spacing, a larger top text size, finer read-aloud controls, native voice entries, a word-by-word Tidy, and a set of AI safety rails**. New fonts, colour filters and reading gimmicks are not on that list. Extra letter spacing is the one typographic change that helps dyslexic readers again and again: it roughly halved errors in children and also helped teens and adults. Lexi has no spacing setting yet. Text-to-speech improves comprehension for readers with reading disabilities (g = 0.35 in a meta-analysis), so read-aloud deserves word highlighting, tap-a-word and a wider speed range. Highlighting itself is a comfort feature, not a proven comprehension boost. Dictation removes the spelling barrier and increases how much people write. That makes a native "voice entry" that keeps the recording the strongest writing upgrade. Spell checking belongs after drafting, one word at a time, with the original always kept. On safety, the APA's November 2025 advisory and new US state laws expect a persistent "this is AI" disclosure, easy-to-find help, crisis handling and data deletion. Lexi has only a US-only keyword version of crisis handling and none of the rest. Dyslexia's link with anxiety and shame (pooled g = −0.54 across 96 studies) is also a reason to retire the "days in a row" streak. Many of Lexi's existing choices are already well supported: Verdana, generous line spacing, the words-only mode, emoji with captions, "Spelling doesn't matter here", and read-aloud on every bubble. The evidence is weakest exactly where Lexi lives, because almost no studies test adults, phones, chat or journaling. Coloured overlays, Bionic Reading, "dyslexia fonts" and syllable colouring should not be built. The native app can technically do nearly everything proposed here. The web artifact cannot record audio, cannot fit neural voices into its 16 MB limit, and gets only patchy word timing from browser voices.

## Spacing, not special fonts, changes how dyslexic readers read

No dyslexia-specific typeface has independent evidence that it improves reading. The studies that look hardest at fonts find that spacing is what matters. In the best-known eye-tracking comparison, 48 people with dyslexia read comparable passages in 12 fonts. **OpenDyslexic took 103% of Arial's reading time, a non-significant difference, and participants significantly preferred Verdana and Helvetica over it** ([Rello & Baeza-Yates 2013](https://www.changedyslexia.org/publications/pdfs/2013-ASSETS-Good%20Fonts%20for%20Dyslexia.pdf)). Children fared no better. In a single-subject study of 11 pupils at a dyslexia school, OpenDyslexic gave "no evidence" of faster or more accurate reading, and no student preferred it ([Wery & Diliberto 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5629233)). Among 170 Dutch children with dyslexia, Dyslexie was neither faster nor more accurate than Arial, and most of the children preferred Arial ([Kuster et al. 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29204931/)). The one apparent win disappears on closer inspection: **Dyslexie's 7% words-per-minute advantage vanished once Arial was given the same letter and word spacing** ([Marinus et al. 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27194598/)). An Italian study that crossed letter shape with spacing found no letter-shape effect at all ([Galliussi et al. 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7188700/)). Lexend and Atkinson Hyperlegible have no peer-reviewed dyslexia studies. Lexend's claims trace back to its designer and promotional pages ([Lexend](https://lexend.com/)), and Atkinson was built for low-vision readers ([CSUN 2024](https://www.csun.edu/cod/conference/sessions/2024/index.php/public/presentations/view/3108.html)). Lexi's Verdana default is defensible because it was the font dyslexic readers liked most in Rello's study. The two alternative fonts are harmless comfort choices, as long as the app never calls them proven.

The lever that does change reading is the space between letters. **When Zorzi and colleagues added 2.5 points of letter spacing to 14-point text (about 0.18 em) for 74 dyslexic children, reading errors roughly halved.** Speed also rose from 1.75 to 1.94 syllables per second, while younger readers matched on reading level gained nothing ([Zorzi et al. 2012](https://ccnl.psy.unipd.it/publications/publications_folder/extra-large-letter-spacing-improves-reading-in-dyslexia/at_download/file)). The effect reaches beyond childhood. Large letter spacing improved accuracy for 24 dyslexic university students ([Sjoblom et al. 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27629067/)) and sped up 32 dyslexic teenagers more than their peers, while coloured overlays in the same study did nothing ([Stagg & Kiss 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34600780/)). Two limits shape the design. First, skilled adult readers without dyslexia showed no change in total reading time, and only 44% read faster ([Perea et al. 2016](https://www.uv.es/mperea/interletter_SJP.pdf)). Second, **widening letter gaps without also widening word gaps slowed reading** ([Galliussi et al. 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7188700/)). The guidance points to the same range. The British Dyslexia Association recommends letter spacing of about 35% of the average letter width, with word spacing at least 3.5 times that ([BDA style guide](https://www.bumc.bu.edu/jmedday/files/2025/05/Dyslexia-friendly-style-guide.docx)). WCAG 1.4.12 requires content to survive user overrides of 0.12 em letter spacing and 0.16 em word spacing, and cites research in which reading speed levelled off near 0.20 em ([W3C 1.4.12](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/text-spacing.html)). A Lexi setting of Normal, Roomy and Extra roomy (roughly +0.05, +0.12 and +0.18 em, each with wider word gaps to match) sits inside the tested range. The benefit tracked weakness in letter identification (r = −0.60 in Zorzi's second experiment), so spacing should be a choice, not a forced default.

Text size matters more than line spacing. In an eye-tracking study of 28 dyslexic readers, **font size changed readability but line spacing did not, and the authors recommended 18 points** ([Rello et al. 2013](https://unpaywall.org/10.1145%2F2461121.2461125)). A later study of 92 people found that larger text and wider character spacing both sped up reading ([Rello & Baeza-Yates 2017](https://repositori.upf.edu/items/e0ca6f40-a0fa-490f-9d03-53d043270deb)). Eighteen points is about 24 px, so Lexi's largest size (23 px, about 17 pt) stops just short of the best-supported value. Lexi's line spacing options (1.5, 1.7 and 2.0) already meet the BDA's 1.5 and need no work. Line length is genuinely contested. The BDA says 60 to 70 characters per line is best ([BDA](https://www.bumc.bu.edu/jmedday/files/2025/05/Dyslexia-friendly-style-guide.docx)). Yet **27 dyslexic high-schoolers read 27% faster (150 vs 118 words per minute) and made less than half as many backward eye movements on a small screen showing about two words per line** ([Schneps et al. 2013, eye tracking](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3734020/)). A larger study of 103 students found gains only in subgroups: weak decoders read faster, and students with limited visual attention span understood more ([Schneps et al. 2013](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0075634)). The lead author estimated that about a third of students had those attention issues ([HealthDay](https://www.healthday.com/health-news/child-health/e-readers-benefit-some-with-dyslexia-study-finds-680220.html)). Lexi's bubbles, at 64 to 70% of a phone screen, hold roughly 18 to 26 characters per line (an estimate for Verdana at 16 to 23 px). That is already well inside the short-line range. A choice between narrow and wide bubbles is worth testing, but it is not a fix.

The evidence on colour is thinner than its popularity suggests. Only one large study exists. It tested **341 adults, 89 with dyslexia, and found that warm backgrounds (peach, orange, yellow) with black text read fastest and cool ones slowest.** The dyslexic group took 14.85 seconds per paragraph on peach and 21.57 seconds on blue-grey, and the colour rankings were almost the same for readers with and without dyslexia (rho = 0.964) ([Rello & Bigham 2017](https://www.changedyslexia.org/publications/pdfs/2017-ASSETS-Good%20Background%20Colors.pdf)). Pure white was never tested, and the study has not been replicated. Lexi's #E6E6E6 light background is a neutral grey close to the study's mid-to-slow "Grey", and its mint bubbles look like the green and turquoise colours that fell in the slower half. Bubbles cover only small areas, so the result does not transfer cleanly. Still, a cream or peach light theme matches both this study and the BDA's advice to use "cream or a soft pastel" ([BDA](https://www.bumc.bu.edu/jmedday/files/2025/05/Dyslexia-friendly-style-guide.docx)). Coloured overlays are a different matter. A systematic review of 51 published studies found effects "generally small and/or similar to the improvement found with a placebo", and concluded that overlays and tinted lenses "cannot be endorsed" ([Griffiths et al. 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27580753/)). Dark mode has no dyslexia trials, and the general research favours dark text on a light background for reading tasks ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/dark-mode/)), so Night and Ember stay as personal preferences. Italics are the one style rule with data behind it: **Arial Italic was the slowest font tested, at 123% of Arial's reading time** ([Rello & Baeza-Yates 2013](https://www.changedyslexia.org/publications/pdfs/2013-ASSETS-Good%20Fonts%20for%20Dyslexia.pdf)). The BDA advises using bold instead of italics or underlining ([BDA](https://www.bumc.bu.edu/jmedday/files/2025/05/Dyslexia-friendly-style-guide.docx)).

Emoji, Lexi's signature, rest more on expert consensus than on proof. The only dyslexia-specific study compared 16 dyslexic and 16 typical readers aged 11 to 15. It called emoticons "tentatively" helpful, while the dyslexic group read every message type more slowly ([Leśniak & Grzybowski 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8374149/)). People also disagree about whether the same emoji is positive, neutral or negative **25% of the time**, and more so across platforms ([Miller et al. 2016](https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/ICWSM/article/view/14757)). W3C's cognitive-accessibility guidance (COGA) supports icons for people with reading difficulties, as long as each icon has one meaning and sits next to its text ([COGA](https://www.w3.org/TR/coga-usable/)). That describes Lexi's captioned tiles exactly, and it is why the words-only mode is good insurance. Two popular "reading aids" fail outright. Bionic Reading did not change eye fixations or speed for 53 eye-tracked adults ([Beelders 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12565662/)), and 2,074 online testers read about 2.6 words per minute slower with it ([Readwise](https://blog.readwise.io/bionic-reading-results/)). A 2025 critical review reports that the benefits of syllable colouring hold up only for first-grade beginners ([Brügelmann 2025](https://www.pedocs.de/volltexte/2025/34472/pdf/Bruegelmann_2025_Red_and_blue.pdf)). The most useful lesson for settings design comes from 352 adults. **Each person's reading speed varied 35% between their fastest and slowest font, and what they preferred did not predict what they read fastest** ([Wallace et al. 2022](https://jeffhuang.com/papers/Readability_TOCHI22.pdf)). Dyslexic readers show the same near-zero link between preference and speed (r = −0.13) ([Rello & Baeza-Yates 2013](https://www.changedyslexia.org/publications/pdfs/2013-ASSETS-Good%20Fonts%20for%20Dyslexia.pdf)). Choice is good for comfort and control, but the defaults have to carry the evidence.

## Read-aloud earns its place, while highlighting is comfort rather than cure

Text-to-speech (TTS) is the reading support with the firmest evidence. A meta-analysis of TTS and read-aloud tools for students with reading disabilities, from third grade through college, found **a comprehension effect of g = 0.35 (95% CI 0.14–0.56)** ([Wood et al. 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5494021/)). There is no comparable evidence for fluency, so Lexi should promise easier understanding, not faster reading. Who benefits also matters. Among 8- to 12-year-olds, both TTS conditions beat silent reading. Children with dyslexia alone gained significantly more than children who also had language difficulties ([Keelor et al. 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37119436/)). That makes users whose main difficulty is decoding, Lexi's core audience, the best-supported beneficiaries. A 30-study meta-analysis (N = 1,945) of reading while listening, mostly in college-age readers, adds a twist. The overall benefit was small (g = 0.18). It appeared only when the audio set the pace (g = 0.41), not when readers set their own pace (g = 0.06) ([Clinton-Lisell 2023](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1403866.pdf)). Lexi's optional auto-read lets the voice lead while the text stays on screen, which is the set-up that finding favours.

Synchronised highlighting is expected but unproven. In the same study of children, **TTS with highlighting did not beat TTS without it** on comprehension ([Keelor et al. 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37119436/)). The only positive data come from small eye-tracking pilots, which suggest children find it easier to read along with a synced highlight ([IAFOR 2015](https://papers.iafor.org/wp-content/uploads/papers/acset2015/ACSET2015_20150.pdf)). Even so, every major tool offers it. Apple can highlight words, sentences or both in a colour the user picks ([Apple](https://support.apple.com/guide/iphone/spoken-content-iph96b214f0/16.0/ios/16.0)), Read&Write and ClaroRead highlight the sentence and the word in two colours ([Read&Write](https://www.everway.com/products/read-and-write-education/); [ClaroRead](https://www.everway.com/en-gb/products/claroread/)), and COGA lists text-to-speech with synchronised highlighting as a recommended support ([COGA](https://www.w3.org/TR/coga-usable/)). Lexi's whole-bubble highlight is the coarsest version. Moving to a shaded sentence with an optional word marker, a colour choice and an off switch matches what dyslexic users meet in other tools. It should be presented honestly as a tracking aid.

Voice quality affects acceptance, not comprehension. Clinton-Lisell found **"no reliable differences" between human and computer-generated voices** in reading-while-listening results ([Clinton-Lisell 2023](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1403866.pdf)). The case for neural voices in Lexi is therefore consistency with its recorded Piper lines and user comfort. Speed is where individual control pays off. In one study of 25 adults with aphasia, the group preferred about 150 words per minute, but individual preferences varied widely ([AJSLP 2020](https://pubs.asha.org/doi/10.1044/2019_AJSLP-19-00047)). Voice Dream offers 50 to 700 words per minute ([MERLOT](https://www.merlot.org/merlot/viewMaterial.htm?id=1170460)), and one dyslexia researcher reports listening at around 650 words per minute alongside a short-line display ([Voice Dream blog](https://www.voicedream.com/how-one-dyslexic-speed-reads/)). Lexi's 0.6 to 1.4 range covers the middle but not fast listeners. A 0.5 to 2.0 range widens it while staying at or below the rate above which Chrome's network voices reportedly stop speaking ([Coder's Block](https://codersblock.com/blog/javascript-text-to-speech-and-its-many-quirks/)).

Other reading features are widely used but untested. Tap-a-word-to-hear and picture dictionaries are standard in Microsoft's Immersive Reader ([Microsoft](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/ai-services/immersive-reader/overview)) and in Read&Write. No controlled study shows that they improve comprehension for dyslexic teens or adults. Tap-to-hear is still a natural, cheap extension of Lexi's speaker button. Reading rulers rest on one conference poster. In it, a fixed underline sped up most dyslexic readers by about 23 words per minute, while a "lightbox" mask slowed some of them by a similar amount ([Adobe/UCF 2021](https://blog.adobe.com/en/publish/2021/05/06/hacking-readability-for-dyslexic-readers)). In a chat made of one-to-three-sentence bubbles, a ruler has little to do. UK students with dyslexia say they most value having assistive technology available "at all times", and 54% used TTS and speech recognition ([Draffan 2014](https://eprints.soton.ac.uk/420256/1/Assistive_Technology_and_Associated_Training_Draffan_2014.pdf)). That favours keeping a speaker on every bubble over building new reading modes.

## Dictation frees the words, and checking belongs after drafting

Speaking instead of typing removes the barrier dyslexic writers feel most. University students with learning disabilities earned **higher overall essay scores with speech recognition than without help**, because spelling no longer held back their choice of longer words ([Higgins & Raskind 1995](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ516141)). Secondary students' dictated essays beat their handwritten ones ([MacArthur & Cavalier 2004](https://nceo.info/references/article-journal/10951)). In a 2024 Nordic single-case study, **7 of 8 students wrote more after speech-to-text training**, and their word-level accuracy held steady or improved ([Almgren Bäck et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1080/17483107.2024.2351488)). A 2025 experiment with 118 learners showed why. Dyslexic learners did worse than their peers when answering by typing, but **the gap disappeared when they answered by speaking**. Modelling traced the typing gap to processing delays, not to memory ([Wilschut et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1111/tops.12769)). Most of this evidence uses older software or school-age students. No rigorous study has tested modern on-device recognition with dyslexic adults.

Dictation swaps spelling errors for technology errors. Screen recordings of four children with severe dyslexia showed no ordinary spelling errors but frequent misheard and dropped words. Most were homophones, wrong word endings and compound words, and recognition accuracy affected how much the children had to hold in working memory ([Svendsen et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1002/dys.70024)). In Google's LaMPost study, 6 of 19 dyslexic adults already used dictation. Others **had given up on dictation because it made more errors than their own typing** ([Goodman et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2207.02308)). Two design responses follow. First, saving the recording next to the transcript makes misrecognitions harmless, because the user's real words survive. That is the case for a native "voice entry". Second, listening back catches dropped words and wrong homophones. Three LaMPost adults checked their emails this way ([Goodman et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2207.02308)), which is why Lexi's existing "Read it back" should stay prominent.

Spell checking helps, but only the right kind at the right time. **Nearly a fifth of dyslexic spelling errors are real words (17% in English and 21% in Spanish texts), and Microsoft Word and LibreOffice did not catch them** ([Rello et al. 2015](https://www.superarladislexia.org/pdf/2015-Luz%20Rello-Spellchecker-assets.pdf)). In Rello's controlled test with 17 dyslexic adults, correction accuracy rose from 78.05 with no help to 89.83 when suspect words were highlighted, and to 93.01 with highlighting plus suggestions. Suggestions were also the fastest condition ([Rello et al. 2015](https://www.superarladislexia.org/pdf/2015-Luz%20Rello-Spellchecker-assets.pdf)). Timing and control matter just as much. A Nordic review found that spellcheckers, word prediction and auto-correct "require time and attention", which disturbs the flow of writing ([Mossige et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1002/dys.1752)). One study of college students reported that real-word errors actually rose among dyslexic students when spellcheck was on ([JoWR](https://www.jowr.org/index.php/jowr/article/download/580/465)), probably because accepting a wrong suggestion turns a misspelling into a wrong real word. Lexi's "Tidy spelling" is already started by the user and kept separate from drafting, which is right. Its weakness is that it can only accept or reject the whole text. Tidy should show each change as its own item the user can tap, with audio and the original always one tap away. For sound-alike pairs it could add an emoji as a meaning cue, which is an untested idea built on Lexi's emoji vocabulary. This applies Rello's highlight-plus-suggestion result without the risks of accepting everything at once. No published benchmark tests AI spelling correction on dyslexic writing, so Lexi should check Claude's Tidy on real samples.

AI writing help leaves users in control only when they make the final choice. LaMPost's dyslexic adults rated an AI "rewrite my selection" tool the most useful feature (5.26 out of 7). They felt in control (5.58 out of 7) when they could filter the options and make the final decision. But the rewrites added **"seemingly relevant yet imaginary details"**. Twelve participants worried about too many choices, and most wanted 3 to 5 options. Seven of the 19 raised concerns about the AI reading and storing personal data ([Goodman et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2207.02308)). People with dyslexia also worry that their mistakes stay visible in edit histories ([Wu et al. 2019](https://ai.meta.com/research/publications/design-and-evaluation-of-a-social-media-writing-support-tool-for-people-with-dyslexia/)). A feelings journal raises these stakes, because the writing is for the self and authenticity matters more than polish. Tidy should fix spelling and sound-alikes only, never tone or content. It should offer at most three choices for any word and keep correction history out of exports.

Behind all of this sits lasting anxiety about writing. Interviews with dyslexic social-media users produced the study's title quote, "I'm never happy with what I write" ([Reynolds & Wu 2018](https://aaai.org/papers/00280-15004-im-never-happy-with-what-i-write-challenges-and-strategies-of-people-with-dyslexia-on-social-media/)). In a Dutch sample of 90 dyslexic and 1,411 typical primary-school pupils, the dyslexic children reported more worry and rumination about spelling ([Dams et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1002/dys.70034)). No study directly tests "spelling doesn't matter" wording or drafting without red squiggles. Both follow from the cost of checking and the fear of visible mistakes, so Lexi's current framing should stay. Writing scaffolds help too. LaMPost participants drafted from bullet points or an unordered "word faucet" ([Goodman et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2207.02308)), which supports tappable, read-aloud sentence starters in free writing. Word prediction improved spelling for six children journaling with phonetic prediction tools ([Evmenova et al. 2010](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ974602)), and 9 of 19 dyslexic adults found predictive text helpful for finding words ([Goodman et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2207.02308)). Prediction also costs attention, so a feeling-word suggester belongs in the experiment tier: opt-in and limited to three options.

## Accessibility standards already spell out Lexi's missing rails

W3C's cognitive-accessibility guidance (COGA) is a Working Group Note that supplements WCAG. It organises its advice into eight objectives, ranging from clear content and error recovery to focus, memory and personalisation ([COGA](https://www.w3.org/TR/coga-usable/)). Lexi's three tabs already fit COGA's yardstick that "a simple application has 3 to 6 functions". That argues for building new features into Home and Journal rather than adding tabs. COGA's language rules give a checkable standard for bubbles: common words (with the 1,500 most common words as a reference), one point per sentence, no double negatives or idioms, and **any paragraph over 50 words split** ([COGA](https://www.w3.org/TR/coga-usable/)). Plain-language guidance suggests an average of 15 to 20 words per sentence ([Somerset Council](https://playbook.somerset.gov.uk/digital-inclusion-and-accessibility/creating-accessible-documents/document-language-and-reading-age)), and GOV.UK once set a limit of 25 ([GOV.UK 2014](https://insidegovuk.blog.gov.uk/2014/08/04/sentence-length-why-25-words-is-our-limit/)). These are conventions, not limits proven in trials. Lexi's scripted lines already follow this style, but Claude's replies vary. Readability formulas measure only surface features ([AHRQ](https://www.ahrq.gov/talkingquality/resources/writing/tip6.html)). The practical fix is an automated check on every AI reply covering sentence and word limits, idioms, double negatives and emoji placement, and removing emoji in words-only mode.

WCAG 2.2 added three criteria aimed at cognition, and one of them matches a Lexi gap directly. **Success criterion 3.2.6, Consistent Help (Level A), requires help that appears on several pages to stay in the same relative place on each one** ([W3C](https://www.w3.org/WAI/standards-guidelines/wcag/new-in-22/)). Lexi has no always-visible help. A Help button in a fixed spot, opening crisis lines and how-to tips, meets both this criterion and the mental-health guidance in the next section. Criterion 3.3.7, Redundant Entry, forbids asking again for information the user already gave in the same process. That supports showing earlier check-in answers instead of re-asking. Criterion 2.5.8 sets a minimum touch target of 24 by 24 px. Lexi's 44 px tiles exceed it, though the Yes and No pills need enough space between them to avoid mis-taps. The timing criteria (2.2.1 and 2.2.6) mean no prompts that expire on their own, and drafts kept for well over 20 hours ([W3C 2.2.1](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/timing-adjustable.html)). Finally, criterion 1.4.12 is a test the web build must pass: when a user applies the spacing overrides, fixed-width bubbles must grow rather than cut off text ([W3C 1.4.12](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/text-spacing.html)).

COGA's memory and navigation patterns describe the guided check-in Lexi should have. Pattern 4.7.5 combines **a summary of earlier steps, a step indicator, an easy way back, and fixing errors without starting over**. Pattern 4.5.2 says going back must "never delete a user's work". Pattern 4.5.9 says to save as the user goes, and 4.5.10 asks for clear status feedback ([COGA](https://www.w3.org/TR/coga-usable/)). For Lexi's flow of a mood followed by nine topics, that means showing "Step 2 of 5" in words plus dots, a Back button on every step, a "Change my answer" link for the last answer, saving each tap instantly, and an editable recap before saving. GOV.UK's dyslexia guidance makes the same point plainly: don't make users remember details from earlier pages, and don't rely on accurate spelling ([GOV.UK 2016](https://accessibility.blog.gov.uk/2016/09/02/dos-and-donts-on-designing-for-accessibility/)).

No study tests chatbot pacing for dyslexic or neurodivergent users, so the design has to come from general standards. COGA says changes should happen only when the user asks, and that users should be able to control interruptions ([COGA](https://www.w3.org/TR/coga-usable/)). That supports a "tap for next message" pace setting, and typing animations that are short, can be skipped and switch off with the phone's reduce-motion setting. There is a tension worth naming here. Response delays and typing cues make chatbots seem more human and raise satisfaction among general users ([Gnewuch et al. 2018](https://aisel.aisnet.org/ecis2018_rp/113/)), but the APA now asks developers of mental-health-adjacent tools to reduce human-like features ([APA advisory](https://www.infocop.es/wp-content/uploads/2025/11/health-advisory-ai-chatbots-wellness-apps-mental-health.pdf)). For emoji, the Nielsen Norman Group says to "always include a visible text label", because most icons lack a standard meaning ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/icon-usability/)). COGA adds that emoji often need an explanation ([COGA](https://www.w3.org/TR/coga-usable/)). Lexi's captions and the Yes/No words on its thumbs-up and thumbs-down pills meet this. The remaining risk is meaning: a thumbs-down in answer to "Did you feel worried?" is ambiguous. Questions should be worded so that "Yes" is the plain answer.

Personalisation and user testing close the loop. COGA asks that users can set font, size, line height and contrast, return to a familiar interface, and reach key settings wherever they need them ([COGA](https://www.w3.org/TR/coga-usable/)). The Fluid Preferences Framework shows one way to save such profiles ([Fluid](https://fluidproject.atlassian.net/wiki/spaces/docs/pages/7079467/Preferences+Framework)). For Lexi, this means a first-run quick start with four or five choices and a live preview, plus a reset button. Settings should be stored as display preferences, not as a disability label. A 2025 scoping review of 117 papers on AI interaction design for autism, ADHD and dyslexia found that **most studies did not include neurodivergent participants** ([Xu et al. 2025](https://researchportal.hw.ac.uk/en/publications/a-scoping-review-of-inclusive-and-adaptive-humanai-interaction-de/)). A CHI 2026 interview study of 23 neurodivergent adults found they use chatbots for emotional regulation and working-memory support, while worrying about authenticity, privacy and masking ([Giri et al. 2026](https://a11y-paradise.onrender.com/reviews/69e7bddb5ba37b5ea06cce97)). Testing with Lexi's own dyslexic users is how to fill the gap the research leaves.

## A shame-prone audience needs an AI that admits it is AI and never keeps score

Lexi's users bring a history that makes design choices emotional. A 2024 meta-analysis of 96 studies and 83,260 participants found that people with reading, maths or other learning difficulties report more anxiety, depression and low self-image than their peers, **with a moderate effect of g = −0.54** ([Vieira et al. 2024](https://acuresearchbank.acu.edu.au/items/04478e26-b53f-4b0c-9fa8-ab8c0ac4bfe2)). A scoping review of 98 studies concluded that reading difficulties tend to come before the emotional symptoms, not after them ([Wilmot et al. 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9864451)). Both reviews draw mostly on young samples. Interviews add the detail. Dyslexic UK university students described hiding their dyslexia, humiliation and self-doubt. A tutor estimated that 75 to 80% of students who referred themselves for screening did not know they were dyslexic, and many said, "I always knew there was something wrong with me" ([Hamilton Clark 2024](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10192362/1/Hamilton%20Clark_Dyslexia%20concealment.pdf)). For many users, writing itself has been a source of shame. That supports Lexi's emoji-first, low-text design, and it argues for onboarding that reassures without assuming a diagnosis.

The benefits of journaling are real but small, and they have not been tested in dyslexic people. **A meta-analysis of 146 expressive-writing studies found an effect of r = 0.075** ([Frattaroli 2006](https://Www.Gwern.net/doc/psychology/2006-frattaroli.pdf)). A 2023 meta-analysis of 31 RCTs (N = 4,012) in healthy and mildly affected groups found g = −0.12 for depression, anxiety and stress combined. Effects were stronger when sessions were 1 to 3 days apart, and the authors did not test whether writing skill changed the results ([Guo 2023, via summary](https://www.simplypsychology.org/expressive-writing-for-anxiety.html)). A UCLA trial found no overall effect, and people's emotional expressiveness affected who benefited ([Niles et al.](https://teams.semel.ucla.edu/sites/default/files/publications/Jan%202014%20-%20Randomized%20controlled%20trial%20of%20expressive%20writing%20for%20psychological%20and%20physical%20health.pdf)). A trial in rheumatoid arthritis found that speaking into a recorder gave different benefits from writing, not the same ones ([PubMed 21315515](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21315515/)). No study has tested journaling, spoken disclosure or emoji entries in dyslexic participants. Lexi should therefore avoid clinical claims and keep the structured check-in as the easy default. Voice entries should be described as "an easier way to get thoughts out", not as therapy.

Naming feelings is the better-supported mechanism for an emoji-first app. Putting a feeling into words, which researchers call affect labelling, works as a quiet form of emotion regulation. It shows up in how people feel, in their bodies and in brain activity, though the mechanisms are still poorly understood ([Torre & Lieberman 2018](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/1754073917742706)). People who tell their negative emotions apart more precisely tend to have less severe anxiety and depression ([Kashdan et al. 2015](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/journals/current-directions/0963721414550708/)). This supports an optional "name it more" step after the emoji tile, in the spirit of How We Feel's 144-word Mood Meter ([How We Feel](https://marcbrackett.com/how-we-feel-app-3/)). For Lexi it should offer only three to five read-aloud words with emoji, to avoid too many choices. Leaving out "Happy" fits research linking habitual acceptance of negative feelings with better mental health ([Quartz on Mauss](https://qz.com/1052751/accepting-negative-emotions-leads-to-better-psychological-health)). No study has tested leaving out a positive option, though, and some users on a genuinely good day may not see themselves in "Content". That is a question for user testing, not a reason to change now.

Expectations for AI safety hardened in 2025. **The APA's November 2025 health advisory asks developers for clear, prominent and persistent disclosure that users are talking to an AI that cannot replace professional care.** It also asks for psychologists' input on safeguards, for detecting and stopping harmful conversations as "a clinical safety issue", and for limits on AI memory and human-like features. Further asks are nudges toward breaks and real-life conversations, transparency about data use with a way to delete data permanently, and never posing as a licensed professional ([APA advisory](https://www.infocop.es/wp-content/uploads/2025/11/health-advisory-ai-chatbots-wellness-apps-mental-health.pdf); [APA press release](https://www.apa.org/news/press/releases/2025/11/ai-wellness-apps-mental-health)). The advisory warns that agreeable, validating AI models can reinforce distorted thinking. That is a sharp risk when a dyslexic user writes "I'm stupid". The law is moving the same way. Illinois banned AI from delivering psychotherapy in August 2025, with fines of up to $10,000 per violation ([Data Privacy & Security Insider](https://www.dataprivacyandsecurityinsider.com/2025/08/illinois-enacts-act-prohibiting-ai-therapy/)), and Utah requires mental-health chatbots to disclose that they are AI ([Blueprint](https://blueprint.ai/blog/breaking-down-current-legislation-regulating-ai-in-mental-health-care)). **California's SB 243, in force since 1 January 2026, requires companion chatbots to disclose that they are AI and to publish a suicide and self-harm protocol that refers users to crisis services.** Users can sue over violations ([Skadden](https://www.skadden.com/insights/publications/2025/10/new-california-companion-chatbot-law); [FPF](https://fpf.org/blog/understanding-the-new-wave-of-chatbot-legislation-california-sb-243-and-beyond/)). Whether Lexi counts as a "companion chatbot" depends on whether it keeps up a relationship across sessions, which needs a legal review. In the UK, the MHRA treats digital mental-health tools as possible medical devices, depending on what they are meant to do ([MHRA 2025](https://www.gov.uk/government/news/digital-mental-health-technologies-guidance-launched-to-help-manufacturers-and-safeguard-users)). Woebot closed its consumer app in June 2025, and its founder blamed the cost of getting FDA authorisation ([STAT](https://statnews.com/2025/07/02/woebot-therapy-chatbot-shuts-down-founder-says-ai-moving-faster-than-regulators)). Measured against all this, Lexi's 988 keyword trigger is a start, but it leaves three gaps. There is no persistent AI label. There is no always-visible help, while Wysa's always-on SOS button is the industry pattern ([Wysa](https://www.wysa.com/for-individuals)). And keyword matching will miss distress written with misspellings or only emoji, a risk nobody has tested but an obvious one for a dyslexic audience. The 988 line also serves only the US.

Streaks are a poor fit for this audience. Across seven studies, **an unbroken streak increased engagement, but people dropped off once a streak broke, especially when they blamed themselves, and streaks that could be "repaired" softened the damage** ([Silverman & Barasch 2023](https://www.colorado.edu/business/faculty-research/2023/04/19/or-track-how-broken-streaks-affect-consumer-decisions)). For users whose central emotional theme is self-blame, a "days in a row" counter is a liability. Apple Journal makes its streak summary something users switch on ([Apple](https://support.apple.com/en-is/guide/iphone/iphf965002cf/ios)), and a total "days journaled" count keeps the motivation without the sense of loss. "Words written" carries the same risk for users who journal with emoji or voice. Reminders should be opt-in, scheduled by the user and free of guilt, as COGA advises ([COGA](https://www.w3.org/TR/coga-usable/)). The 1-to-3-day spacing from the journaling meta-analysis is a reasonable gentle rhythm. Export completes the picture. Competitors such as Daylio offer PDF or CSV export ([Daylio](https://daylio.net/)), Bearable lets users "export and delete everything at any time" ([Bearable](https://bearable.app/symptom-tracker/)), and the APA asks for permanent deletion. A simple, low-text PDF summary to share with a doctor and a one-tap "delete everything" are overdue.

## Native Lexi can ship nearly everything, but the web artifact hits three walls

Word-level highlighting is possible on both platforms, through three routes. For Lexi's 58 pre-recorded lines, the cleanest route is to do the work in advance. The maintained Piper fork (GPL-3.0, run only at build time) can output per-phoneme timings with `include_alignments=True` when the voice model includes alignment data ([piper1-gpl voice.py](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/OHF-Voice/piper1-gpl/main/src/piper/voice.py)). These can be grouped into word timings and shipped as small JSON files next to each clip. The highlight then follows the playback position the same way on web and native. For dynamic text the picture is messier. The browser `boundary` event has only partial support in Chrome ([caniuse](https://caniuse.com/mdn-api_speechsynthesisutterance_boundary_event)). It has been reported not to fire on Android Chrome and to work only with local voices ([Coder's Block](https://codersblock.com/blog/javascript-text-to-speech-and-its-many-quirks/); [W3C list](https://lists.w3.org/Archives/Public/public-speech-api/2021Jun/0000.html)), and Safari leaves out the word length. Lexi needs to detect support while running and fall back to an estimate based on a timer. Drawing the highlight is easy, because the CSS Custom Highlight API ships in Chrome and Edge 105, Firefox 140 and Safari 17.2 ([caniuse](https://caniuse.com/mdn-api_highlight)). On native, expo-speech connects `onBoundary` to both iOS and Android in its source code, but its docs make no platform guarantee, and `pause()` and `resume()` do not work on Android ([Expo Speech](https://docs.expo.dev/versions/latest/sdk/speech/)).

Natural voices for dynamic text split sharply by platform. **The smallest Kokoro model is 86 MB, and Piper voices are 63 to 114 MB** ([Hugging Face Kokoro](https://huggingface.co/api/models/onnx-community/Kokoro-82M-v1.0-ONNX?blobs=true); [Piper voices](https://huggingface.co/api/models/rhasspy/piper-voices/tree/main/en/en_US/lessac?recursive=true)). The web artifact has a 16 MB page limit and network restrictions, so the web build stays with pre-recorded clips plus the best local system voice. On native, react-native-sherpa-onnx (MIT, with some LGPL parts) runs Piper, Kokoro and other models on the device, with streaming and word timings ([sherpa-onnx RN](https://github.com/XDcobra/react-native-sherpa-onnx)). Kokoro's Apache-2.0 licence is the cleanest ([Kokoro](https://github.com/hexgrad/kokoro)). The model would be a large but workable first-run download. Cloud TTS is cheap: OpenAI's tts-1 costs $15 per million characters ([OpenAI](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/pricing)). But it sends journal text to a second company, and ElevenLabs offers zero data retention only on enterprise plans ([ElevenLabs](https://elevenlabs.io/docs/api-reference/text-to-speech/convert-with-timestamps)). A cheaper native win is pointing users to iOS "Enhanced" voices, which expo-speech can list, and telling them that iPhones stay silent in silent mode ([Expo Speech](https://docs.expo.dev/versions/latest/sdk/speech/)). Lexi should also confirm the licence of its exact Piper voice file. Piper's voice documentation says some voices have restrictive licences, and it describes the project as intended for personal use and research ([Piper VOICES.md](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/OHF-Voice/piper1-gpl/main/docs/VOICES.md)). iOS Personal Voice needs a permission request that expo-speech does not provide ([Dodson 2024](https://bendodson.com/weblog/2024/04/03/using-your-personal-voice-in-an-ios-app/)), and Apple aims it mainly at communication-aid apps ([WWDC23 notes](https://wwdcnotes.com/documentation/wwdc23-10033-extend-speech-synthesis-with-personal-and-custom-voices/)), so it can wait.

Dictation upgrades are mostly a matter of settings. **expo-speech-recognition 58.0.0, published 16 October 2026, supports on-device recognition, automatic punctuation, custom vocabulary hints, and saving the recorded audio on Android 13+ and iOS** ([expo-speech-recognition](https://github.com/jamsch/expo-speech-recognition); [npm](https://registry.npmjs.org/expo-speech-recognition)). That covers everything a voice entry needs, although a release only one day old should be tested for early bugs. On-device Whisper through whisper.rn is a fallback only if the phone's own dictation disappoints. Its models are 32 to 488 MB each ([whisper.rn](https://github.com/mybigday/whisper.rn); [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/api/models/ggerganov/whisper.cpp/tree/main)). Published speech-recognition failures involve speech conditions such as dysarthria and stuttering, not dyslexia ([ICLR 2025](https://www.iclr.cc/virtual/2025/34325)), so there is no evidence that dyslexic speakers need a special recogniser. The web artifact has no microphone, and Chrome's recogniser sends audio to a server anyway ([MDN](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/API/SpeechRecognition)).

Phone accessibility settings and fonts are easy wins. React Native reports reduce motion and screen-reader status on both platforms, and the text size setting through `PixelRatio.getFontScale()` ([RN AccessibilityInfo](https://reactnative.dev/docs/accessibilityinfo); [RN PixelRatio](https://reactnative.dev/docs/pixelratio)). Lexi should never turn off font scaling, and should cap only dense controls with `maxFontSizeMultiplier` ([RN Text](https://reactnative.dev/docs/text)). On the web, `prefers-reduced-motion` works in all current browsers ([caniuse](https://caniuse.com/mdn-css_at-rules_media_prefers-reduced-motion)). Atkinson Hyperlegible Next was released in February 2025 with seven weights and support for more than 150 languages ([Ophthalmology Management](https://ophthalmologymanagement.com/news/2025/applied-design-and-braille-institute-partner-to-launch-typeface-for-making-reading-easier/)). It is on Google Fonts at about 12 KB per weight ([jsDelivr](https://data.jsdelivr.com/v1/packages/npm/@fontsource/atkinson-hyperlegible-next@5.3.0?structure=flat)), so swapping it in is simple. One check matters. Expo lists Roboto as Android's default font ([Expo fonts](https://docs.expo.dev/develop/user-interface/fonts/)), and React Native accepts a single font name with no backup list ([RN Text](https://reactnative.dev/docs/text)). Unless Verdana is bundled with the app, the native default probably shows as Roboto on Android. Spell checking can stay with Claude's Tidy. If needed, it can be backed by local libraries such as nspell and Double Metaphone, which find sound-alike candidates without a connection ([nspell](https://registry.npmjs.org/nspell/latest); [double-metaphone](https://registry.npmjs.org/double-metaphone/latest)).

| Upgrade | Web artifact | Native app (Expo SDK 58) |
|---|---|---|
| Letter and word spacing | CSS `letter-spacing` and `word-spacing`; pass the WCAG 1.4.12 test | Letter spacing set in points from the font size; word spacing may need a workaround (to verify) |
| Word highlight on Lexi's 58 recorded lines | Word timings made at build time from Piper, synced to audio position | Same timing files, synced to playback position |

| Fonts | Google Fonts link (Lexend, Atkinson Hyperlegible Next) | Bundle with expo-font; check Verdana on Android |

## Conclusion

The research changes Lexi's roadmap from "add dyslexia features" to "add dials and safety rails". Lexi's instincts already sit on the right side of nearly every finding: Verdana, emoji with words, short bubbles, read-aloud everywhere and no spelling pressure. The popular dyslexia features it lacks, such as special fonts, tints, Bionic text and syllable colours, are the ones the evidence rejects. What Lexi is missing are dials whose value differs from person to person (spacing, size, speed, pace) and safety rails that a vulnerable audience and new laws now demand (an AI label, consistent help, crisis handling that copes with misspellings, deletion). The biggest risks come from features Lexi already has that pull against the evidence. A streak counter and a word count keep score on users who carry shame from school. Human-like warmth, such as typing dots, celebrations and an avatar, is something the APA now asks mental-health-adjacent tools to tone down.

The second insight is that the evidence runs out exactly where Lexi operates. Nearly every typography study used children, non-English text, paper or desktop screens, and no study tests TTS, spacing, emoji or journaling in a phone chat with dyslexic adults. That makes Lexi's own users the missing data. Build the first-tier dials as user choices, then watch which settings dyslexic testers actually pick and whether they keep journaling. That would produce evidence the field does not have, as long as it is collected with the same plain-language consent and privacy the journal itself requires.

## Features list for Lexi

Each feature below has a short name, what it does, why (with evidence strength and a key source), where it runs, rough effort and status. Evidence labels: **Strong** means a meta-analysis or several replicated studies. **Moderate** means one or more good controlled studies. **Limited** means small, single, indirect or tool-maker evidence. **Standard** means W3C, APA, law or an official style guide. **Myth** means reviews show no real benefit. Effort: **S** = a few days, **M** = one to three weeks, **L** = a month or more. Status: **New** (Lexi doesn't have it), **Improve** (change something Lexi has) or **Keep** (already right). Everything marked "test" should go to a small panel of dyslexic testers first.

| **Sound-alike check** | Tidy flags words like their/there or hear/here, with an emoji and audio for each meaning. | **Moderate:** about 1 in 5 dyslexic errors are real words that checkers miss ([Rello 2015](https://www.superarladislexia.org/pdf/2015-Luz%20Rello-Spellchecker-assets.pdf)). The emoji cues are untested. | Both | S | Improve |
| **Sentence starters** | Tappable, read-aloud starters like "Today I felt…" in free writing. | **Limited:** dyslexic adults start from bullet points and outlines ([LaMPost 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2207.02308)). | Both | S | New |
| **Name it more** | After a mood emoji, an optional step with 3 to 5 exact feeling words, each with emoji and audio. | **Moderate (general population):** naming feelings calms them ([Torre & Lieberman 2018](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/1754073917742706)); more exact feeling words go with better coping ([Kashdan 2015](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/journals/current-directions/0963721414550708/)). | Both | M | New |
| **Gentle reminders** | Opt-in reminders at a time you pick, in kind words, never "you missed a day". | **Standard** ([COGA 4.8.7](https://www.w3.org/TR/coga-usable/)). **Moderate:** sessions 1 to 3 days apart worked best ([Guo 2023](https://www.simplypsychology.org/expressive-writing-for-anxiety.html)). | Native | M | New |
| **Voice help** | If only robotic voices exist, show how to get a better one; add a "Can't hear? Check silent mode" tip. | **Technical:** iOS "Enhanced" voices exist, and silent mode mutes speech ([Expo Speech](https://docs.expo.dev/versions/latest/sdk/speech/)). | Both | S | Improve |

### 5. Don't build: myth or too little evidence

| Idea | Why not | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| **Coloured overlays or "Irlen" tints** | Effects match placebo. Colour themes are fine for comfort, never as treatment. | **Myth** ([Griffiths 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27580753/); [Stagg & Kiss 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34600780/)) |
| **Bionic Reading** | No change in speed or eye movements; slightly slower in a big online test. | **Myth** ([Beelders 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12565662/); [Readwise](https://blog.readwise.io/bionic-reading-results/)) |
| **"Dyslexia fonts" as a fix** | Don't add OpenDyslexic or Dyslexie, or call Lexend or Atkinson proven. They gave no speed or accuracy gain, and Dyslexie's small edge came from spacing. | **Myth** ([Wery & Diliberto 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5629233); [Kuster 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29204931/); [Marinus 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27194598/)) |
| **Syllable colours** | Helps beginners, not older readers; hyphen tools don't find true syllables. | **Limited** ([Brügelmann 2025](https://www.pedocs.de/volltexte/2025/34472/pdf/Bruegelmann_2025_Red_and_blue.pdf); [Hypher](https://github.com/bramstein/hypher)) |
| **Reading ruler** | One small poster with mixed results, and Lexi's bubbles already have short lines. | **Limited** ([Adobe/UCF 2021](https://blog.adobe.com/en/publish/2021/05/06/hacking-readability-for-dyslexic-readers)) |
| **Auto-correct or red lines while writing** | Checking costs attention and breaks flow, and spellcheck raised real-word errors. | **Moderate** evidence against ([Mossige 2023](https://doi.org/10.1002/dys.1752); [JoWR](https://www.jowr.org/index.php/jowr/article/download/580/465)) |
| **AI rewriting** | AI rewrites added made-up details, and a journal needs the user's own words. | **Limited** ([LaMPost 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2207.02308)) |
| **"Lexi remembers you" memory** | The APA says to limit AI memory, and long-running relationships may bring in California's companion-chatbot rules. | **Standard** ([APA 2025](https://www.infocop.es/wp-content/uploads/2025/11/health-advisory-ai-chatbots-wellness-apps-mental-health.pdf); [SB 243](https://www.skadden.com/insights/publications/2025/10/new-california-companion-chatbot-law)) |
| **Therapy or "proven" claims** | Illinois bans AI therapy, and the UK may treat such apps as medical devices. | **Standard** ([Illinois](https://www.dataprivacyandsecurityinsider.com/2025/08/illinois-enacts-act-prohibiting-ai-therapy/); [MHRA 2025](https://www.gov.uk/government/news/digital-mental-health-technologies-guidance-launched-to-help-manufacturers-and-safeguard-users)) |
| **AI or cloud voices in the web version** | Voice models are 86 to 325 MB, against a 16 MB page limit, and cloud voices send journal text to another company. | **Technical** ([Kokoro](https://huggingface.co/api/models/onnx-community/Kokoro-82M-v1.0-ONNX?blobs=true); [ElevenLabs](https://elevenlabs.io/docs/api-reference/text-to-speech/convert-with-timestamps)) |
| **Dictation in the web version** | The artifact has no microphone, and Chrome sends audio to a server anyway. | **Technical** ([MDN](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/API/SpeechRecognition)) |
| **"Read in my own voice" (Personal Voice)** | Not yet: it needs native code that expo-speech lacks, and Apple aims it at communication-aid apps. | **Technical** ([Dodson 2024](https://bendodson.com/weblog/2024/04/03/using-your-personal-voice-in-an-ios-app/); [WWDC23 notes](https://wwdcnotes.com/documentation/wwdc23-10033-extend-speech-synthesis-with-personal-and-custom-voices/)) |
