# Visual and interaction design evidence for dyslexic and neurodivergent users (for Lexi's UI upgrade)

Grading key used on every finding: **[Strong]** = multiple peer-reviewed studies or large controlled study with consistent results; **[Moderate]** = one or two decent peer-reviewed studies, or consistent but small studies; **[Weak]** = small/single/indirect studies, mixed results, or non-dyslexic populations extrapolated; **[Expert consensus only]** = style guides, standards or practitioner guidance with no cited trials behind the specific number.

Source-verification note: items marked "(fetched)" were read in full or in part this session; items marked "(search summary)" come from search-result abstracts/snippets; items marked "(not re-fetched this session)" are well-known primary sources cited from prior knowledge with their canonical URLs and should be spot-checked by the report writer before quoting exact numbers.

## 1. Contrast, glare, off-whites/off-blacks, visual stress, and dark mode

### Takeaway
There is no good evidence that pure black-on-white is *worse* for dyslexic readers than black on a tinted background, because the main study (Rello & Bigham 2017) never tested white; the "high contrast vibrates" claim traces back to non-peer-reviewed web articles. What *is* evidenced is that warm, light tints (peach, orange, yellow) beat cool tints (blue, blue-grey, green) for reading speed in *both* dyslexic and typical readers, and that dark-text-on-light (positive polarity) beats light-on-dark in the general population; there is no dyslexia-specific dark-mode evidence at all.

### Cited Findings
- **[Moderate]** Rello & Bigham, "Good Background Colors for Readers: A Study of People with and without Dyslexia" (ASSETS 2017): 341 participants (89 with dyslexia), within-subjects, 10 background colours, all with pure black text (#000000), 18-point Arial, left-aligned, 55-word Spanish texts; readability measured by reading time and mouse distance, comprehension used only as a control. Warm backgrounds Peach, Orange and Yellow significantly improved reading performance over Blue, Blue Grey and Green. (fetched) — [Rello & Bigham 2017 PDF](https://www.changedyslexia.org/publications/pdfs/2017-ASSETS-Good%20Background%20Colors.pdf)
- **[Moderate]** Exact colours and contrast ratios tested (hex, ratio vs black text): Peach #EDD1B0 14.35:1; Orange #EDDD6E 15.17:1; Yellow #F8FD89 19.4:1; Grey #D8D3D6 14.21:1; Turquoise #A5F7E1 16.99:1; Red #E0A6AA 10.2:1; Purple #B987DC 7.56:1; Blue #96ADFC 9.68:1; Green #A8F29A 15.83:1; Blue Grey #DBE1F1 16.05:1. All exceeded 7:1 (WCAG AAA). **White and off-white were not tested**; the authors list comparing Peach/Orange/Yellow against "white or off-white" as future work. (fetched) — [Rello & Bigham 2017 PDF](https://www.changedyslexia.org/publications/pdfs/2017-ASSETS-Good%20Background%20Colors.pdf)
- **[Moderate]** Effect sizes (mean reading time, dyslexia group): Peach 14.85 s (baseline 100%), Orange 15.33 s (103%), Yellow 16.30 s (109%), neutral light Grey 18.05 s (121%), Blue 19.42 s (130%), Blue Grey 21.57 s (145%). Control group showed the same ordering (Peach 12.28 s vs Blue Grey 18.82 s, 153%). Correlation between groups' colour rankings: rho = 0.964 for reading time. I.e. the effect is **not dyslexia-specific**. (fetched) — [Rello & Bigham 2017 PDF](https://www.changedyslexia.org/publications/pdfs/2017-ASSETS-Good%20Background%20Colors.pdf)
- **[Moderate]** The authors themselves caution that the results "do not necessarily mean that warm colors are recommended", that cool colours are often used to calm and some readers may prefer them, and that reading preference was not measured. (fetched) — [Rello & Bigham 2017 PDF](https://www.changedyslexia.org/publications/pdfs/2017-ASSETS-Good%20Background%20Colors.pdf)
- **[Weak / opinion]** The often-repeated claim that high contrast "creates so much vibration that it diminishes readability" and the advice to pair off-black text with off-white backgrounds come from Perron (2012, personal website) and Bradford (2011, dyslexia-parent magazine), as cited in Rello & Bigham's related-work section; neither is a controlled study. (fetched) — [Rello & Bigham 2017 PDF](https://www.changedyslexia.org/publications/pdfs/2017-ASSETS-Good%20Background%20Colors.pdf)
- **[Moderate]** Earlier eye-tracking work (22 dyslexic participants, then replicated with 92 participants, 46 dyslexic) found black text on a cream background produced the shortest mean fixation durations among the pairs tested. (fetched, as summarised in Rello & Bigham) — [Rello & Bigham 2017 PDF](https://www.changedyslexia.org/publications/pdfs/2017-ASSETS-Good%20Background%20Colors.pdf)
- **[Expert consensus only]** BDA-derived guidance: avoid white backgrounds because white "can appear too dazzling"; use cream or a soft pastel; note that some dyslexic people have their own colour preference; use single-colour backgrounds without patterns or pictures. (search summary of an older BDA-based guide hosted by Wigan Council; the 2023 BDA PDF itself returned 403/404 this session) — [Wigan BDA-based Dyslexia Style Guide](https://www.wigan.gov.uk/Docs/PDF/Business/Professionals/SEN/HEFA/Dyslexia-Style-Guide.pdf); [Ako Aotearoa Dyslexia-Friendly Style Guide 2023, citing BDA 2023](https://ako.ac.nz/assets/Knowledge-centre/ALNACC-Resources/Dyslexia-resources/230907-Dyslexia-Friendly-Style-Guide.pdf) (fetched: "Use single-colour backgrounds and avoid background patterns or pictures")
- **[Strong — against]** Coloured overlays/lenses (the Meares-Irlen "visual stress" model): Griffiths et al. 2016 systematic review graded studies with the Cochrane risk-of-bias tool; most were at "high" or "uncertain" risk of bias, effects were generally small and/or similar to placebo, and results did not differ by colour system. (search summary) — [Griffiths et al. 2016, White Rose eprint](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/104441/)
- **[Strong — against]** Suttle et al. 2018 overview of systematic reviews: 3 of 4 reviews found insufficient good-quality evidence to support coloured overlays or lenses for reading difficulty; "there is not yet a reliable evidence base". (search summary) — [Suttle et al. 2018 PDF](https://city-test.eprints-hosting.org/id/eprint/19242/1/Efficacy%20of%20Coloured%20Overlays%20and%20Lenses%20for%20the%20Treatment%20of%20Reading%20Difficulty.pdf)
- **[Strong — against]** Miyasaka et al. 2019 Irlen syndrome systematic review: high heterogeneity and "lack of evidence on the existence of IS and treatment effectiveness". (search summary) — [Arquivos de Neuro-Psiquiatria 2019](https://thieme-connect.de/products/ejournals/abstract/10.1590/0004-282X20190014)
- **[Moderate]** Positive polarity advantage (general population, not dyslexia-specific): Piepenbrock, Mayr and Buchner found reading/proofreading was faster and more accurate with dark text on light backgrounds than light on dark; proposed mechanism is pupil constriction under brighter displays reducing optical blur; the advantage is largest for fine detail (small text) and persists in older adults. (search summary) — [Piepenbrock et al. abstract (Fundación Mapfre catalogue)](https://documentacion.fundacionmapfre.org/documentacion/publico/es/bib/150136.do); [Piepenbrock, Mayr, Mund & Buchner, younger and older adults](https://documentacion.fundacionmapfre.org/documentacion/publico/pt/bib/143702.do?format=xml); nuance on the pupil explanation: [Mathôt & Ivanov 2019, PeerJ](https://peerj.com/articles/8220)
- **[Weak]** No peer-reviewed study testing dark mode with dyslexic readers was found. The nearest is an unpublished Croatian master's thesis (46 typical readers, mobile) where dark grey #121212 reduced reading errors and dark blue #15202B was fastest among dark backgrounds. (search summary) — [Univ. North (Croatia) thesis](https://repozitorij.unin.hr/islandora/object/unin:7125/datastream/PDF)
- **[Expert consensus only]** Material Design's dark-theme guidance uses #121212 as the dark surface instead of pure black and renders high-emphasis text at about 87% white opacity, to reduce glare/halation and keep elevation visible. (not re-fetched this session) — [Material Design 2 dark theme](https://m2.material.io/design/color/dark-theme.html)
- **[Expert consensus only / standard]** WCAG contrast floors: 4.5:1 for normal text and 3:1 for large text (1.4.3, AA); 7:1 / 4.5:1 for AAA (1.4.6). WCAG 1.4.8 Visual Presentation (AAA) also requires that users can select foreground and background colours. (not re-fetched this session) — [WCAG 2.2 Understanding 1.4.6](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/contrast-enhanced.html); [WCAG 2.2 Understanding 1.4.8](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/visual-presentation.html)

### Inferences
- Lexi's cream theme is directionally supported (warm light tint, black-ish text), but the evidence does **not** justify dropping contrast low. Every condition in Rello & Bigham was 7.5:1 to 19.4:1, so there is no evidence about contrast in the 4.5–7:1 range that "soft/premium" palettes often drift into. Safe rule: body text at or above ~10–12:1, never below 7:1; warm off-white page (light cream/ivory), near-black warm text (for example a dark brown-black rather than #000).
- Because the colour effect is the same for dyslexic and typical readers, a warm-neutral default is a universal design choice rather than a "special needs" signal, which helps the premium/adult positioning.
- Rello & Bigham's tints are fairly saturated (#EDD1B0, #F8FD89). A premium palette would use much lighter warm neutrals; there is no direct evidence these lighter creams beat white, so frame this as "low-risk, evidence-consistent", not "proven".
- Default to a light theme (positive polarity advantage). Offer a dark theme as an option for comfort and night journaling, built on a dark grey (around #121212–#1C1A18) with off-white text, not pure #000/#FFF. Keep tinted-theme choice user-selectable (WCAG 1.4.8) but do not market tints/overlays as a dyslexia treatment.

### Gaps
- No study found comparing white vs off-white vs cream directly for dyslexic readers on screens.
- No study found on dark mode, light-on-dark text, or halation specifically in dyslexic readers; "pure white on black causes halation for astigmatism" is widely repeated but no primary source was located this session.
- The 2023 BDA Dyslexia Style Guide PDF could not be retrieved (403/404); its exact wording on contrast and dark mode is unverified here.
- No evidence found on whether the optimal contrast differs for short chat bubbles vs long passages.

## 2. Layout and density: line length, whitespace, alignment, chunking, crowding (including UI element spacing)

### Takeaway
Shorter lines help a subgroup of struggling readers (those with visual-attention/crowding-type difficulties) by cutting regressions, with no comprehension cost; standards cap lines at 80 characters and require ragged-right text. Crowding is real and larger in dyslexia on average, and extra letter spacing helps some readers, but the benefit is not dyslexia-specific, varies a lot between individuals, and can slow fast readers, so spacing should be a tunable setting with moderate defaults rather than maximal by default.

### Cited Findings
- **[Moderate]** Schneps et al. 2013, "Shorter Lines Facilitate Reading in Those Who Struggle" (PLoS ONE 8(8): e71161): on a small handheld device, short lines cut regressive eye movements by more than half with no loss in comprehension; proposed mechanism is reducing crowded text to the left of gaze. (search summary) — [DOAJ record](https://doaj.org/article/497468bb821f4887ae8f17e195b471f9); [Harvard DASH](https://dash.harvard.edu/handle/1/11855772)
- **[Moderate]** Schneps et al. 2013, "E-Readers Are More Effective than Paper for Some with Dyslexia" (PLoS ONE 8(9): e75634): 103 high-school students with dyslexia; speed and comprehension benefits appeared only for particular subgroups (those with limited visual attention span), while students without visual-attention difficulties did better with conventional text; the authors attribute the benefit to the short-line format rather than the device. (search summary) — [Smithsonian release](https://dashboard.si.edu/newsdesk/releases/smithsonian-experts-find-e-readers-can-make-reading-easier-those-dyslexia); [InfoDocket summary](https://www.infodocket.com/2013/09/19/new-research-paper-e-readers-are-more-effective-than-paper-for-some-with-dyslexia/)
- **[Moderate]** Zorzi et al. 2012 (PNAS), "Extra-large letter spacing improves reading in dyslexia": Italian and French dyslexic children read faster and with substantially fewer errors (reported as roughly halved) when inter-letter spacing was enlarged, with no training; the authors attribute this to dyslexic readers being abnormally affected by crowding. Exact spacing values and the reading-level control results should be checked in the full text (fetch blocked by a captcha this session). (search summary) — [Zorzi et al. 2012, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3396535)
- **[Moderate]** Martelli et al. 2009 (Journal of Vision), "Crowding, reading, and developmental dyslexia": dyslexic children show larger critical spacing (more crowding) and their reading rate is limited by it. (not re-fetched this session) — [Martelli et al. 2009, Journal of Vision 9(4):14](https://doi.org/10.1167/9.4.14)
- **[Moderate — complicating]** Korinth et al. 2020 (Frontiers in Psychology), "Wider letter-spacing facilitates word processing but impairs reading rates of fast readers": letter-spacing effects were not mediated by individual crowding sensitivity (neither foveal nor parafoveal crowding correlated with the spacing benefit), and wider spacing slowed fast readers' text reading. Commentary in this line of work argues the original dyslexia-specific claim misread a non-significant control-group effect as absence of an effect. (search summary) — [Korinth et al. 2020, PMC](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7090332/)
- **[Weak]** Tijms and colleagues (BDA International Conference 2016 abstract): dyslexic and typical readers benefited equally from extra-large letter spacing, and crowding appeared to act more at the inter-word than inter-letter level. Conference abstract only. (search summary) — [BDA 2016 conference abstract](https://bdainternationalconference2016.sched.com/event/6ECD/visual-crowding-in-normal-and-dyslexic-children-who-benefits-from-extra-letter-spacing-and-why)
- **[Weak]** An eye-tracking naming study found dyslexic readers' gaze durations were longer in foveal and parafoveal crowding conditions than peripheral, suggesting a broader crowding span. (search summary) — [Bangor University record](https://research.bangor.ac.uk/portal/en/researchoutputs/naming-fluency-in-dyslexic-and-nondyslexic-readers-differential-effects-of-visual-crowding-in-foveal-parafoveal-and-peripheral-vision(428f3c14-dacb-4640-a5a2-f50701caf464)/export.html)
- **[Moderate]** Rello & Baeza-Yates (Universal Access in the Information Society, 2015/2017), eye tracking with 92 participants (46 dyslexic): larger text and larger character spacing led both dyslexic and non-dyslexic participants to read significantly faster. (search summary) — [UPF repository record](https://repositori.upf.edu/items/e0ca6f40-a0fa-490f-9d03-53d043270deb)
- **[Moderate]** Rello, Pielot and colleagues ("Size matters (spacing not)", W4A 2013, 28 dyslexic participants; and the follow-up "Make it big!" CHI 2016): font size significantly affected readability and comprehension, line spacing did not, and they recommend 18 points for web text; the smallest and largest line spacings were associated with poorer comprehension answers. An earlier 2012 layout study (14–26 pt) favoured 26 pt. (search summary) — [Change Dyslexia blog summary](https://blog.changedyslexia.org/?p=345); [BYU Editing Research summary](https://editingresearch.byu.edu/?p=10398)
- **[Expert consensus only / standard]** WCAG 1.4.8 Visual Presentation (AAA): lines no wider than 80 characters, text not justified, line spacing at least 1.5 within paragraphs, paragraph spacing at least 1.5 times the line spacing, text resizable to 200% without horizontal scrolling. (not re-fetched this session) — [WCAG 2.2 Understanding 1.4.8](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/visual-presentation.html)
- **[Expert consensus only / standard]** WCAG 1.4.12 Text Spacing (AA): content must not break when users set line height to 1.5x font size, paragraph spacing to 2x font size, letter spacing to 0.12x, and word spacing to 0.16x. (not re-fetched this session) — [WCAG 2.2 Understanding 1.4.12](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/text-spacing.html)
- **[Expert consensus only]** BDA-derived guidance: left-aligned, ragged-right text; avoid narrow newspaper-style columns; 1.5 line spacing; extra space around headings and between paragraphs to avoid clutter; break text into short paragraphs and chunk with headings; boxes and borders can help highlight important information. (fetched: Ako 2023; search summary: Wigan) — [Ako Aotearoa 2023 guide](https://ako.ac.nz/assets/Knowledge-centre/ALNACC-Resources/Dyslexia-resources/230907-Dyslexia-Friendly-Style-Guide.pdf); [Wigan BDA-based guide](https://www.wigan.gov.uk/Docs/PDF/Business/Professionals/SEN/HEFA/Dyslexia-Style-Guide.pdf)
- **[Expert consensus only]** W3C COGA "Making Content Usable" includes patterns "Use White Spacing", "Avoid Too Much Content", "Keep Text Succinct" and "Break Media into Chunks". (fetched, titles) — [W3C COGA Making Content Usable](https://www.w3.org/TR/coga-usable/)

### Inferences
- Chat is structurally good for dyslexic readers: bubbles are naturally short-line, chunked text. Protect this: cap bubble width at roughly 45–65 characters at the user's chosen size (well under WCAG's 80), and on wide/desktop layouts keep the conversation column narrow and centred rather than stretching.
- Rello's "18 points" may mean 18pt (24 CSS px) or was reported loosely; either way, Lexi's 16px minimum is below the evidence-backed size. Consider defaulting to about 20–22px for body text in bubbles with 16px reserved for metadata, and keeping 28px available.
- Spacing (letter, word, line) should stay user-adjustable with moderate defaults (for example letter spacing around 0.02–0.05em, word spacing modestly increased, line height 1.5), since big spacing helps some readers but slows others (Korinth 2020).
- Crowding evidence is about letters and words, but it is a reasonable (unproven) extension to UI: avoid dense grids where emoji tiles, labels and buttons sit tightly together; use generous gaps (at least 8–12px) between tiles and between a label and its neighbour; avoid placing text over busy imagery.
- Whitespace is the main lever that both makes the UI look "premium" and matches dyslexia guidance; the premium look should come from spacing and restraint rather than from shrinking text or lowering contrast.

### Gaps
- No study found that directly tests UI element spacing (buttons/tiles) for dyslexic users; the UI-spacing recommendation is extrapolation from text-crowding research.
- The exact BDA 2023 line-length figure (commonly quoted as 60–70 characters) could not be verified this session because the PDF was inaccessible.
- No meta-analysis of letter-spacing effects in dyslexia was found; the literature is mixed.
- No evidence found on the optimal number of messages or items visible on screen at once for dyslexic users.

## 3. Visual hierarchy: weight, size contrast, emphasis, number of type sizes

### Takeaway
Guidance consistently says: headings at least 20% larger than body, bold for emphasis, and avoid italics, underlining (except links) and ALL CAPS; Rello's font studies found italics measurably hurt reading. A refined, minimal type scale is compatible with this, provided body text stays large and steps between levels are at least about 1.2x.

### Cited Findings
- **[Expert consensus only]** BDA 2023-based guidance (via Ako Aotearoa, which cites BDA 2023): headings at least 20% larger than body text, with bold for further emphasis; avoid or limit italics and underlines; avoid capital letters for whole words and headings ("Graphic designers often love using capitalisation, which makes words harder for some people to read"); add extra space around headings. (fetched) — [Ako Aotearoa 2023 guide](https://ako.ac.nz/assets/Knowledge-centre/ALNACC-Resources/Dyslexia-resources/230907-Dyslexia-Friendly-Style-Guide.pdf)
- **[Expert consensus only]** Older BDA-based guide: underlining and italics make text appear to run together; use bold instead. (search summary) — [Wigan BDA-based guide](https://www.wigan.gov.uk/Docs/PDF/Business/Professionals/SEN/HEFA/Dyslexia-Style-Guide.pdf)
- **[Moderate]** Rello & Baeza-Yates "Good fonts for dyslexia" (ASSETS 2013, eye tracking with dyslexic readers): sans-serif, roman (upright) and monospaced fonts performed well while italic fonts had a negative effect on reading performance. (search summary) — [BYU Editing Research summary](https://editingresearch.byu.edu/?p=10398)
- **[Moderate]** Specialist "dyslexia fonts" do not improve reading: Wery & Diliberto 2017 (OpenDyslexic, small sample of students) found no improvement in reading rate or accuracy; Kuster et al. 2018 (Dyslexie font, Dutch children with and without dyslexia) found no benefit. (not re-fetched this session) — [Wery & Diliberto 2017, Annals of Dyslexia](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11881-016-0127-1); [Kuster et al. 2018, Annals of Dyslexia](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11881-017-0154-6)
- **[Expert consensus only]** COGA "Use a Consistent Visual Design" (consistent heading styles, control appearance, focus indicators) and "Make the Purpose of Your Page Clear" (clear heading/signpost). (fetched) — [W3C COGA Making Content Usable](https://www.w3.org/TR/coga-usable/)

### Inferences
- A premium, minimal type system fits the evidence: one typeface family per theme, three or four sizes total (for example body, small/meta, title, optional large display), a scale ratio around 1.2–1.33 so each step clears the "20% larger" rule, and two weights (regular plus semibold/bold). Hierarchy should come from size, weight and space, never italics or tracking-wide uppercase labels.
- Small uppercase "eyebrow" labels and light/thin font weights are common "sophisticated" tropes; both conflict with the guidance (caps) and with legibility (thin strokes reduce effective contrast). Use sentence case and regular-or-heavier weights.
- Because "dyslexia fonts" show no benefit, OpenDyslexic can remain as a choice (preference matters for comfort) but should not be the default or the visual identity; Lexend or Atkinson Hyperlegible give a more contemporary look without losing legibility claims (the font-level evidence is weak either way).
- Underline should be reserved for links, if used at all; the per-word read-aloud highlight should be a background highlight rather than an underline.

### Gaps
- No controlled study found on the number of type sizes or heading-to-body size ratios for dyslexic readers; the 20% figure is guidance only.
- No study found comparing bold vs colour vs background highlight for emphasis in dyslexic readers.
- No study found on font weight (light vs regular vs medium) for dyslexic readers.

## 4. Iconography: emoji vs custom icons vs line icons, labels, size, consistency

### Takeaway
Icons and images that support text are recommended by dyslexia guidance, but icon-only UI is risky: only a handful of icons are universally recognised, and the same emoji is interpreted differently by different people about a quarter of the time and differently again across platforms. The evidence favours pairing every emoji/icon with a visible word, using one consistent rendered emoji set, and generous touch targets.

### Cited Findings
- **[Moderate]** Miller, Thebault-Spieker et al. (GroupLens, ICWSM 2016), "'Blissfully happy' or 'ready to fight': Varying Interpretations of Emoji": for the same emoji rendering, participants disagreed on whether the sentiment was positive, neutral or negative about 25% of the time, and disagreement increased when the same emoji was compared across platform renderings (for example iPhone vs Samsung). (search summary, secondary reporting) — [NPR/KQED coverage](https://www.kqed.org/pop/22314/lost-in-translation-study-finds-interpretation-of-emojis-can-vary-widely); [Improbable Research](https://improbable.com/2016/04/11/the-trouble-with-emoji-misinterpreted-emotions/)
- **[Expert consensus only / moderate usability research]** Nielsen Norman Group icon-usability research: icons without labels perform worse on comprehension, particularly non-standard ones; only a few icons (home, print, search magnifier) are near-universally recognised; NN/g's 2015–2016 studies found hidden navigation lowered engagement and task success and increased time on task. (search summary of secondary sources citing NN/g; original article not retrieved) — [NN/g Icon Usability](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/icon-usability/); [secondary summary](https://trevorcalabro.substack.com/p/every-icon-needs-a-label)
- **[Weak — counterpoint]** Some studies find icons speed visual search compared with text for familiar items, and an eye-tracking study found labels can sometimes slow interaction; the effect depends on icon familiarity. (search summary) — [UX Movement](https://uxmovement.com/mobile/why-you-shouldnt-always-label-your-icons/); [Human Factors in Design journal](https://periodicos.udesc.br/index.php/hfd/article/view/19376)
- **[Expert consensus only]** Dyslexia style guidance: use images to support text, including flowcharts, arrows and icons. (fetched) — [Ako Aotearoa 2023 guide](https://ako.ac.nz/assets/Knowledge-centre/ALNACC-Resources/Dyslexia-resources/230907-Dyslexia-Friendly-Style-Guide.pdf)
- **[Expert consensus only]** W3C COGA patterns "Use Icons that Help the User", "Include Symbols and Letters" and "Clearly Identify Controls". (fetched, titles) — [W3C COGA Making Content Usable](https://www.w3.org/TR/coga-usable/)
- **[Expert consensus only / standard]** Touch target size: WCAG 2.5.8 (AA) minimum 24x24 CSS px (or equivalent spacing); WCAG 2.5.5 (AAA) 44x44 CSS px. Platform guidance: Apple 44x44 pt, Material 48x48 dp. (not re-fetched this session) — [WCAG 2.2 Understanding 2.5.8](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/target-size-minimum.html); [WCAG 2.2 Understanding 2.5.5](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/target-size-enhanced.html)

### Inferences
- Keep emoji as the core mood/topic vocabulary (it is the product's identity and reduces reading load), but always show a visible one-word label under each tile (which can also be tapped to hear it). This directly addresses the ~25% sentiment-ambiguity finding and helps users with weak emoji fluency.
- Use one bundled emoji set (for example a single open-source set rendered as images/SVG) across web, iOS and Android so meanings and visual style stay consistent; native OS emoji vary by platform and age and look less controlled, which also undermines the "premium" look.
- For UI chrome (settings, send, listen, back), a single consistent line-icon family with labels on primary actions looks more refined than emoji used as UI icons; reserve emoji for emotional content. Keep icon stroke weight sturdy (not hairline) for legibility.
- Tile sizes well above minimums (for example 64–88px tiles with 8–12px gaps) are justified for motor co-occurrence and crowding concerns; large tiles do not need to look childish if they are restrained (neutral surfaces, subtle borders, no saturated rainbow fills).

### Gaps
- No study found specifically on emoji comprehension in dyslexic or low-literacy adults.
- No study found comparing emoji vs custom illustration vs line icons for dyslexic users.
- The NN/g icon article itself was not retrieved; figures attributed to it (for example a 42% increase in clicks from labelling a hamburger icon) could not be verified and are omitted from findings.

## 5. Color: coding, palette size, pastel vs saturated, background tints, overlays

### Takeaway
Warm light tints are mildly evidence-backed as backgrounds (for everyone), coloured overlays as a dyslexia treatment are not, and colour must never be the only carrier of meaning. Saturated, multi-colour "bright" designs are specifically flagged as stigmatising in assistive tech for dyslexia.

### Cited Findings
- **[Moderate]** Warm tints (Peach #EDD1B0, Orange #EDDD6E, Yellow #F8FD89) gave faster reading than cool tints (Blue Grey #DBE1F1 slowest, 45% slower for dyslexic readers than Peach); same pattern for typical readers. (fetched) — [Rello & Bigham 2017 PDF](https://www.changedyslexia.org/publications/pdfs/2017-ASSETS-Good%20Background%20Colors.pdf)
- **[Strong — against]** Coloured overlays and lenses: effects generally small or placebo-like, studies mostly high/uncertain risk of bias; three of four systematic reviews find insufficient evidence; Ritchie et al. (61 schoolchildren with reading difficulties) found no immediate benefit of Irlen overlays. (search summary) — [Griffiths et al. 2016](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/104441/); [Suttle et al. 2018](https://city-test.eprints-hosting.org/id/eprint/19242/1/Efficacy%20of%20Coloured%20Overlays%20and%20Lenses%20for%20the%20Treatment%20of%20Reading%20Difficulty.pdf); [Miyasaka et al. 2019](https://thieme-connect.de/products/ejournals/abstract/10.1590/0004-282X20190014)
- **[Expert consensus only]** BDA-derived guidance: single-colour backgrounds, no patterns or pictures behind text; cream or soft pastel instead of white; some dyslexic people have personal colour preferences. (fetched/search summary) — [Ako Aotearoa 2023 guide](https://ako.ac.nz/assets/Knowledge-centre/ALNACC-Resources/Dyslexia-resources/230907-Dyslexia-Friendly-Style-Guide.pdf); [Wigan BDA-based guide](https://www.wigan.gov.uk/Docs/PDF/Business/Professionals/SEN/HEFA/Dyslexia-Style-Guide.pdf)
- **[Weak]** A systematic review of assistive technologies for children with dyslexia notes that tools designed for learning disabilities with large fonts and bright backgrounds can attract social stigma and discourage use. (search summary) — [arXiv 2412.13241 systematic review](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2412.13241)
- **[Expert consensus only / standard]** WCAG 1.4.1 Use of Color: colour must not be the only visual means of conveying information. (not re-fetched this session) — [WCAG 2.2 Understanding 1.4.1](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/use-of-color.html)
- **[Expert consensus only]** Early user studies (Gregor & Newell, ASSETS 2000) found dyslexic participants chose varied, idiosyncratic colour pairs (for example "mucky green/brown" and blue/yellow), supporting user choice over a single prescribed palette. (fetched, as cited in Rello & Bigham) — [Rello & Bigham 2017 PDF](https://www.changedyslexia.org/publications/pdfs/2017-ASSETS-Good%20Background%20Colors.pdf)

### Inferences
- A calm premium palette is compatible with the evidence: warm neutral background, near-black warm text, one primary accent colour, one or two semantic colours (for example success/error), and muted/desaturated tints for surfaces. Avoid rainbow tile fills; let the emoji supply colour.
- If mood or topic tiles are colour-coded, pair colour with emoji plus label, and keep the colour secondary (border or soft background), never the only signal.
- Present theme colours as comfort preferences, not as treatment; avoid language like "reduces visual stress" or "overlay mode" which implies an unsupported clinical claim.

### Gaps
- No study found on pastel vs saturated UI palettes for dyslexic users, or on how many distinct colours are optimal.
- The 2023 BDA guide's exact colour advice (including any colours to avoid) was not retrievable this session.
- No evidence found on whether emotion-colour mappings (for example blue for sad) help or hinder mood selection.

## 6. Motion and animation: vestibular issues, attention, ADHD co-occurrence

### Takeaway
ADHD (especially inattention) co-occurs with reading disability well above chance, and dyslexia and ADHD share a processing-speed weakness, so unnecessary motion and auto-advancing content are a real cost. Standards require pausable auto-moving content and recommend letting users switch off interaction-triggered motion; short, purposeful feedback animation is acceptable. There is no dyslexia-specific animation research.

### Cited Findings
- **[Moderate]** Willcutt & Pennington 2000 (Journal of Learning Disabilities), community twin sample of 494 twins with reading disability and 373 without: individuals with RD were significantly more likely to meet ADHD criteria, with a stronger association for inattention than hyperactivity-impulsivity; parent and teacher reports agreed, so symptoms were not just academic frustration. (search summary) — [Scholarhub record of Willcutt & Pennington 2000](https://scholarhub.vn/publication/Comorbidity-of-Reading-Disability-and-Attention-Deficit-Hyperactivity-Disorder/63fc18e8-f8b3-41da-be2d-7bba2c68575e); [CU Boulder record](https://vivo.colorado.edu/display/pubid_52783)
- **[Moderate]** Boada, Willcutt & Pennington 2012: dyslexia and ADHD are each among the most prevalent complex neurodevelopmental disorders, each affecting roughly 5% of the US population. (search summary) — [Boada et al. 2012 PDF](https://alliedhealth.ceconnection.com/files/UnderstandingtheComorbidityBetweenDyslexiaandAttentionDeficitHyperactivityDisorder-1352212337316.pdf)
- **[Moderate]** Shared processing-speed deficit: twin analyses found reading disability and ADHD share a processing-speed weakness that is primarily due to common genetic influences (Shanahan et al.). (search summary) — [Shanahan et al., Processing Speed Deficits in ADHD and Reading Disability](https://mijn.bsl.nl/processing-speed-deficits-in-attention-deficit-hyperactivity-dis/536786)
- **[Weak — conflicting]** A 2026 Cognitive Neuroscience Society poster (over 1,000 children) found no support for processing speed or working memory as a *shared* deficit and that reading disorder drove most of the extra impairment in comorbid children; conference abstract, not peer reviewed. (search summary) — [CNS 2026 poster](https://www.cogneurosociety.org/poster/?id=6885)
- **[Expert consensus only / standard]** WCAG 2.2.2 Pause, Stop, Hide (A): moving, blinking or scrolling content that starts automatically and lasts more than 5 seconds must be pausable/stoppable. WCAG 2.3.3 Animation from Interactions (AAA): motion animation triggered by interaction can be disabled unless essential, because it can trigger vestibular symptoms (dizziness, nausea) and distraction. (not re-fetched this session) — [WCAG 2.2 Understanding 2.2.2](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/pause-stop-hide.html); [WCAG 2.2 Understanding 2.3.3](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/animation-from-interactions.html)
- **[Expert consensus only]** The prefers-reduced-motion media query reports the OS-level "reduce motion" setting so sites can remove or replace non-essential motion. (not re-fetched this session) — [MDN prefers-reduced-motion](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/CSS/@media/prefers-reduced-motion)
- **[Expert consensus only]** COGA patterns: "Ensure Controls and Content Do Not Move Unexpectedly", "Let Users Control When Content Moves or Changes", "Limit Interruptions", "Provide Feedback". (fetched, titles) — [W3C COGA Making Content Usable](https://www.w3.org/TR/coga-usable/)
- **[Expert consensus only]** NN/g guidance: most UI animations should be roughly 100–500 ms; longer becomes a distraction or delay. (not re-fetched this session) — [NN/g Animation Duration](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/animation-duration/)

### Inferences
- Good motion for Lexi: short (about 150–250 ms) opacity/scale feedback on tile selection, a gentle fade/slide-in for new bubbles, and a calm typing indicator; these help continuity and confirm actions. Bad motion: parallax, bouncing or looping emoji, confetti bursts, auto-scrolling while the user is reading, and animated backgrounds.
- Honour prefers-reduced-motion on web and the OS reduce-motion flag on iOS/Android (React Native AccessibilityInfo.isReduceMotionEnabled), replacing movement with simple cross-fades; also expose an in-app "reduce motion" toggle.
- Lexi's message-pace setting is a good fit with "let users control when content moves"; additionally, do not auto-scroll a new bubble into view if the user has scrolled up to reread, and never auto-advance read-aloud highlighting without a visible pause control.
- Calm and premium aesthetics and low-motion design point the same way, so this is not a trade-off.

### Gaps
- The commonly quoted "25–40% of people with dyslexia also have ADHD" figure could not be verified from a primary source this session; the report should say "substantially elevated" unless the writer can verify.
- No study found on animation effects for dyslexic users specifically, or on micro-interaction feedback for neurodivergent users.
- No data found on what fraction of dyslexic users enable reduced-motion settings.

## 7. Cognitive load and working memory: progressive disclosure, fewer choices, consistency, recognition over recall

### Takeaway
Dyslexia is associated with weaker verbal working memory and slower processing speed (often shared with ADHD), so the interface should minimise what users must hold in mind, show fewer options at a time, keep controls in fixed familiar places, and make every step and its state visible. Most of the interface-level guidance here is expert consensus (W3C COGA), not trials.

### Cited Findings
- **[Moderate]** Theoretical review of ten dyslexia-ADHD comorbidity studies: most support a multiple cognitive deficit model in which both conditions share weaknesses in working memory, processing speed and other functions. (search summary) — [Journal review (ATU)](https://jpe.atu.ac.ir/article_12102_en.html)
- **[Moderate]** Working memory: adults with dyslexia show deficits in verbal (phonological loop) working memory and in central executive tasks, not only phonological processing (Smith-Spark & Fisk 2007). (not re-fetched this session) — [Smith-Spark & Fisk 2007, Memory](https://doi.org/10.1080/09658210601043384)
- **[Expert consensus only]** W3C COGA "Making Content Usable" patterns: "Use a Familiar Hierarchy and Design" (common layouts and components, buttons that look and act as expected, option to revert to a previous interface version), "Use a Consistent Visual Design" (consistent placement of common features), "Make Each Step Clear" (show completed, current and pending steps so users can reorient after distraction), "Do Not Rely on Users' Calculations or Memorizing Information", "Let Users Go Back", "Make it Easy to Undo Form Errors", "Avoid Data Loss and Timeouts", "Avoid Too Much Content", "Limit Interruptions". The document is a W3C Working Group Note supplemental to WCAG 2.2, built on user research and personas rather than quantified trials. (fetched) — [W3C COGA Making Content Usable](https://www.w3.org/TR/coga-usable/)
- **[Expert consensus only]** COGA user story on cognitive stress: users need simple, consistent content that avoids mental fatigue and makes them feel safe. (fetched) — [W3C COGA Making Content Usable](https://www.w3.org/TR/coga-usable/)
- **[Moderate usability research]** NN/g studies (2015–2016) found hidden navigation (for example hamburger menus) reduced discoverability, engagement and task success and increased time on task compared with visible navigation. (search summary citing NN/g) — [secondary summary](https://trevorcalabro.substack.com/p/every-icon-needs-a-label)
- **[Expert consensus only]** Dyslexia-friendly teaching/resource guidance: chunk learning, use visual prompts, give clear explicit instructions, clearly indicate the "must-do" parts. (fetched) — [Ako Aotearoa 2023 guide](https://ako.ac.nz/assets/Knowledge-centre/ALNACC-Resources/Dyslexia-resources/230907-Dyslexia-Friendly-Style-Guide.pdf)

### Inferences
- Progressive disclosure: show a short primary set of mood/topic tiles (for example 6–9) with a clear "More" route rather than one large grid; keep the advanced settings (spacing, speed, pace, fonts) behind a single, consistently placed settings entry with live preview.
- Recognition over recall: keep last-used mood/topic and settings visible; show the chosen emoji+label in the conversation so users do not need to remember earlier choices; make read-aloud and tap-to-hear affordances persistent on every bubble rather than discovered through gestures.
- Fixed control positions (send, listen, mic, settings) across every screen; avoid hiding primary actions in overflow menus; one primary action per screen.
- Make destructive or irreversible actions undoable (for example deleting an entry), and never time out a journaling session or lose drafts.
- "Premium" minimalism aligns with this when it removes clutter; it conflicts when it hides labels, relies on gestures, or uses icon-only controls. Minimal should mean fewer things, not fewer cues.

### Gaps
- No study found that tests specific choice-set sizes (for example 6 vs 12 tiles) with dyslexic users.
- No experimental study found on progressive disclosure or navigation patterns specifically with dyslexic adults; recommendations rest on COGA consensus and general usability research.
- A current meta-analysis quantifying working-memory effect sizes in adult dyslexia was not retrieved this session.

## 8. Aesthetics, perceived usability, trust, and stigma for disabled and neurodivergent users

### Takeaway
Assistive technology that visibly marks users as disabled, or looks like it is "for special needs", carries social costs that drive non-use, and "large fonts and bright backgrounds" are specifically named as stigmatising for dyslexia tools; mainstream-looking, discreet tools are preferred. Separately, more attractive interfaces tend to be *perceived* as more usable (aesthetic-usability effect), though the direction of causality is contested.

### Cited Findings
- **[Moderate]** Shinohara & Wobbrock, "In the Shadow of Misperception: Assistive Technology Use and Social Interactions" (CHI 2011), interviews with 20 people with disabilities: specific assistive devices sometimes marked users as having disabilities; functional access usually outweighed self-consciousness; two pervasive misperceptions (devices eliminate disability; users are helpless without them); the authors argue AT design must be assessed for social and professional impact. (search summary) — [Shinohara & Wobbrock 2011 PDF](https://faculty.washington.edu/wobbrock/pubs/chi-11.03.pdf); [Shinohara dissertation, Design for Social Accessibility](https://digital.lib.washington.edu/researchworks/bitstreams/27dfb99c-758b-47a1-bc80-daf494f17a96/download)
- **[Weak]** Systematic review of AT for children with dyslexia: tools built for learning disabilities with large fonts and bright backgrounds can draw social stigma and discourage use. (search summary) — [arXiv 2412.13241](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2412.13241)
- **[Weak / practitioner]** Students with dyslexia and other high-incidence disabilities often felt embarrassed by visible AT, partly because they were the only ones using it; smartphones and tablets let them use text-to-speech without standing out; discreet mainstream apps lower the barrier to accepting help. (search summary) — [Don Johnston summary of prior study](https://learningtools.donjohnston.com/2021/04/the-case-against-at-does-accessibility-even-matter); [AdLit, Stigma and Assistive Technology](https://www.adlit.org/ask-the-experts/todd-cunningham/stigma-and-assistive-technology); [Tes](https://www.tes.com/magazine/archive/issue-empower-learners-accept-help)
- **[Weak]** Research on doctoral students and workplace contexts documents real and perceived stigma among adults with dyslexia and argues for co-design. (search summary) — [Eileen Healy, University of Limerick](https://www.idc.ul.ie/?p=2895); [UL publication](https://pure.ul.ie/en/publications/what-to-consider-when-exploring-multidimensional-stigmatisation-a/)
- **[Moderate]** AT abandonment: Phillips & Zhao 1993 found about 29% of assistive devices were abandoned, with predictors including lack of consideration of user opinion in selection and poor device performance. (not re-fetched this session) — [Phillips & Zhao 1993, Assistive Technology](https://doi.org/10.1080/10400435.1993.10132205)
- **[Moderate]** Tractinsky, Katz & Ikar 2000 ("What is beautiful is usable", Interacting with Computers 13(2):127–145), ATM-simulation experiment: perceived aesthetics and perceived usability were strongly correlated before use and the correlation persisted after use; aesthetics influenced post-use usability judgments while actual usability did not. (search summary) — [Interacting with Computers abstract](https://academic.oup.com/iwc/article-abstract/13/2/127/898608); [Tractinsky 2000 PDF](https://mycourses.aalto.fi/pluginfile.php/1645430/mod_folder/content/0/Tractinsky%20%282000%29.pdf)
- **[Moderate — contradicting]** Tuch et al. 2012 manipulated usability and aesthetics independently in online shops and found aesthetics did not affect perceived usability, but degraded usability lowered perceived aesthetics, i.e. the reverse direction; a critique also noted the Tractinsky layouts may have differed in real usability. (search summary) — [InfoQ summary](https://www.infoq.com/news/2012/05/Aesthetics-Usability); [Monk & Lelos 2007 PDF](https://www-users.york.ac.uk/~am1/HOIT07MonkLelos.pdf)
- **[Weak / practitioner]** CAST profile of a high-school student with dyslexia who sometimes viewed AT as "cheating" rather than support, illustrating identity concerns around visible accommodations. (search summary) — [CAST](https://www.cast.org/resources/assistive-technology-for-dyslexia-a-students-journey-to-confidence-and-access/)

### Inferences
- The owner's instinct is supported: a "low budget" or child-like look is not just cosmetic; it risks signalling "special needs tool", which the stigma literature links to non-use by teens and adults. A journaling app is also used privately but is often opened in public (on a phone), so it should look like a mainstream wellness/journaling app at a glance.
- The specific stigmatising markers named in the evidence (large fonts plus bright backgrounds) mean Lexi should keep large text but drop bright, saturated, primary-colour styling. Large text in a restrained palette with generous whitespace reads as "editorial/premium" rather than "remedial".
- Accessibility settings should be framed as personalisation ("Reading comfort") rather than disability accommodations, and should be the same for everyone; this mirrors the evidence that background colour and font-size effects are universal (Rello & Bigham; Rello & Baeza-Yates).
- Because the aesthetic-usability causal direction is contested, the safest bet is to improve both: polish that also reduces friction (consistent spacing, alignment, fewer elements) rather than decoration.
- Co-design or at least usability testing with dyslexic teens and adults is supported by the abandonment literature (user opinion in selection predicts continued use).

### Gaps
- No study found that directly measures how visual polish affects trust, engagement or stigma for dyslexic users of a specific app, or for emoji-first interfaces with adults.
- No evidence found on whether emoji-first design reads as childish to adult users; this is a design risk to test, not an established finding.
- Kurosu & Kashimura 1995 (the original apparent-usability study) and NN/g's aesthetic-usability article were not retrieved this session.
- No evidence found on "calm" visual design reducing anxiety in journaling or mental-wellbeing apps for neurodivergent users.
