# Writing and input support for dyslexic people, applied to Lexi (feelings journal)

Evidence-strength key used below: **[Strong]** = replicated controlled studies or a systematic review; **[Moderate]** = one or more small controlled or single-case experimental studies; **[Limited]** = qualitative studies, design studies, or surveys; **[Very limited]** = a single small study, a snippet-only source, or an indirect population; **[Vendor]** = a claim made by a product's maker or a listing, not independently tested. Age group is noted on each finding. **CHILD-ONLY** marks findings from children under 13 only.

Method note: I read the full text of Rello et al. (2015) and LaMPost (Goodman et al., 2022). The other peer-reviewed items come from abstracts (Europe PMC, ERIC) or search snippets, and these are marked "abstract" or "snippet". Several publisher pages (Taylor & Francis, Wiley, PubMed) blocked full-text retrieval.

## 1. Speech-to-text / dictation for dyslexic writers: quality, quantity, pitfalls, best practice

### Takeaway
Dictation reliably removes the spelling barrier. It tends to increase how much people write, and it often improves rated quality compared with handwriting or unaided writing. The benefit is highly individual, though, and it depends on recognition accuracy and on training. Dictation replaces spelling errors with *technology errors*: misrecognised words, dropped words and homophones. Those errors add a separate correction step that loads working memory. Most of the evidence is old (1990s and 2000s software) or comes from school-aged students. Rigorous adult studies of modern ASR are close to nonexistent.

### Cited Findings
**Quality and quantity**
- University students with LD (n=29) wrote essays under three conditions: unaided, with a human transcriber, and with speech recognition (SR). They scored significantly higher holistic scores with SR than unaided. Text analysis suggested SR encouraged longer words, i.e. richer vocabulary that was no longer limited by spelling. **[Moderate; adults; pre-2010, 1990s discrete-speech SR]** — [Higgins & Raskind 1995, Learning Disability Quarterly (ERIC)](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ516141)
- In MacArthur & Cavalier (2004, *Exceptional Children*), essays by students with LD that were dictated to SR were not as good as those dictated to a human scribe, but they were better than their handwritten essays. Students without LD performed the same in all three conditions. A secondary summary reports that about two-thirds (68%) of students reached 85% recognition accuracy. **[Moderate; secondary-school students; pre-2010; the 68% figure comes from a secondary summary]** — [NCEO reference for MacArthur & Cavalier 2004](https://nceo.info/references/article-journal/10951); [Learning Technologies AB research summary](https://www.learningtechnologiesab.com/speech-recognition/what-the-research-says/)
- Earlier dictation research on 4th and 6th graders with LD found that dictated stories were longer, contained fewer grammatical errors and were rated higher in quality than handwritten or word-processed ones. **[Moderate; CHILD-ONLY; pre-2010; snippet]** — [Composing via Dictation and Speech Recognition Systems (ResearchGate)](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/258166163_Composing_via_Dictation_and_Speech_Recognition_Systems_Compensatory_Technology_for_Students_with_Learning_Disabilities)
- Nelson & Reynolds (2015) observed five postsecondary students who used or were learning SR for college composition. SR was effective for *some* students with chronic spelling difficulties, and several students dropped out of the study. A subset of successful SR users showed a "strong aversion to formal planning", although some planning still happened. **[Limited; adults; qualitative, n=5]** — [Nelson & Reynolds 2015, J. Postsecondary Education and Disability (ERIC)](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1074665)
- A scoping review on adolescents with learning difficulties in secondary school (2022) reports that texts written with speech-to-text had significantly fewer errors than handwritten texts. Less fluent writers produced more words and fewer errors with STT. Results across studies were mixed: one study found better word-level accuracy but *no* gain in length compared with keyboarding, and another found fewer surface errors along with longer narratives. **[Moderate; teens; snippet only, full text blocked]** — [Scoping review, Disability and Rehabilitation: Assistive Technology 2022](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/17483107.2022.2149865)
- In a 2024 Nordic multiple-baseline study, eight middle-school students (Norwegian and Swedish) took part in an intensive STT intervention. Seven of the eight increased text productivity. Word-level accuracy was maintained or improved, and punctuation improved in students with poor baseline skills. Most STT texts were at least equal to keyboarded texts in meaningfulness and quality. The authors argue that removing the spelling barrier let students develop higher-order skills such as vocabulary diversity. They also note that visible progress (longer texts) may motivate continued use, though gains were not always immediate and varied a lot between students (three showed the largest effects). **[Moderate; young teens; single-case design; abstract]** — [Almgren Bäck et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1080/17483107.2024.2351488)
- In an experiment with N=118 learners, those with dyslexia performed worse than typical learners when answering by *typing*, but the gap disappeared when they answered by *speech*. Modelling showed the typing disadvantage came from processing delays, not poorer memory. **[Moderate; learners (age not stated in the abstract); not a writing-composition task]** — [Wilschut, Sense & van Rijn 2025, Topics in Cognitive Science](https://doi.org/10.1111/tops.12769)
- In LaMPost's sample of 19 adults with dyslexia, "verbal communication was said to be easier than writing", and six participants used speech-to-text to dictate their thoughts. Others had *abandoned* dictation because of errors: one said the software's error rate was higher than their own typing errors. **[Limited; adults; qualitative]** — [Goodman et al. 2022, LaMPost (arXiv)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2207.02308)

**Pitfalls**
- Svendsen et al. (2026) recorded screencasts of four 10 to 12-year-olds with severe dyslexia using STT. They found *no conventional spelling errors*, but frequent technology-based errors: mostly misrecognised single words and omitted words or parts of sentences. Three error categories were identified: homophones or near-homophones, wrong suffixes or morphemes, and compound words. Evaluation split into correction, low-level revision and high-level revision. The extra correction step is caused by the technology, and recognition accuracy "appears to influence writing flow and working memory load". **[Limited; CHILD-ONLY; qualitative, n=4; abstract]** — [Svendsen et al. 2026, Dyslexia](https://doi.org/10.1002/dys.70024)
- A study of 15 college students with LD found some evidence that SR improved writing, but its benefits were "highly variable" and depended on many factors. The search summary did not specify those factors, and I am not certain which of these two pages carries the claim. **[Limited; adults; secondary summary, attribution uncertain]** — [LD Online: Speech Recognition for Learning](https://www.ldonline.org/ld-topics/assistive-technology/speech-recognition-learning); [Learning Technologies AB research summary](https://www.learningtechnologiesab.com/speech-recognition/what-the-research-says/)
- An early review warned of a "significant discrepancy" between how SR products are advertised and students' actual experience, and said new products must be checked carefully. **[Limited; adults; pre-2010]** — [McNaughton 1998 (ERIC)](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ575676)
- Adults in LaMPost described a "word faucet" strategy (n=5): getting everything out as an unordered "blob of text", typed or dictated, and organising it later. The researchers tried to have an LLM restructure such dictated blobs into ordered paragraphs, but dropped the feature because chaining was slow and stripped sentences of context. **[Limited; adults]** — [Goodman et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2207.02308)

**Best practice**
- Recommended practice (not peer-reviewed): dictate in short sections and review each one, because shorter chunks make recognition errors easier to find. Then listen to the finished draft with text-to-speech to catch missing words and wrong homophones. **[Vendor]** — [VoiceDash: Speech to Text for Dyslexia (vendor blog)](https://voicedash.ai/speech-to-text-for-dyslexia/)
- To check emails, three of LaMPost's dyslexic adults listened to their message through text-to-speech, and four asked another person to read it. **[Limited; adults]** — [Goodman et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2207.02308)
- The Nordic intervention found that explicit, systematic STT instruction plus progress monitoring was needed, and that later sessions had to balance productivity against formal accuracy (punctuation, conventions). **[Moderate; teens]** — [Almgren Bäck et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1080/17483107.2024.2351488)

### Inferences
- For a feelings journal, the main goal is *getting thoughts out*, so dictation's strongest effect (more words, richer vocabulary, no spelling block) is exactly the one that matters, and its weakness (formal accuracy) matters least. This suggests treating a dictated entry as finished as-is, without pushing the user to correct it.
- Technology errors (homophones, dropped words) are the new "spelling errors" of dictation. Lexi's "Tidy" step could target them specifically: flag low-confidence or homophone words from the speech recogniser and offer read-back using Lexi's recorded or natural voice. This follows the "listen back" practice reported by adults.
- Saving the *audio* alongside the transcript (a voice entry) makes misrecognitions harmless, because the user's real words are preserved. This also addresses the LaMPost users who abandoned dictation because of errors.
- Chunked dictation (short bursts, with each chunk shown and optionally played back) fits the evidence that shorter segments make errors easier to spot, and it reduces working-memory load.

### Gaps
- I found no controlled study of modern neural ASR (Whisper-class, Apple or Google on-device) with dyslexic *adults* measuring writing quality or quantity. Most adult evidence is from 1995 to 2015 software.
- I did not retrieve Apple or Google documentation on automatic punctuation, voice editing commands ("comma", "new line", "delete that") or confidence display. These need checking against current iOS and Android docs.
- I found no study of voice *editing* (correcting dictated text by voice) for dyslexic users.
- I could not get the full texts of the 2022 scoping review or MacArthur & Cavalier (2004), so effect sizes and exact sample ages are unconfirmed.

## 2. Spell checkers: generic vs dyslexia-specific, and UI patterns

### Takeaway
Generic spell checkers miss a large share of dyslexic errors. They miss *real-word errors* (form/from, there/their) and severe or phonetic misspellings where the intended word is far from the typed one. Dyslexia-specific approaches (context-based, phonetic, homophone-aware) detect more, and a 2015 controlled study showed dyslexic users corrected text more accurately *and faster* when shown suggestions. Every checking feature costs time and attention, though, and interrupts the flow of writing. Checking is best kept separate from drafting.

### Cited Findings
- In Rello, Ballesteros & Bigham's "Real Check" (ASSETS 2015), nearly 20% of errors made by people with dyslexia are real-word errors: 17% in English and 21% in Spanish texts (citing Pedler 2007 and Rello et al. 2012). Common checkers such as Microsoft Word 2013 and LibreOffice did not detect them. **[Moderate; adults; full text read]** — [Rello et al. 2015, A Spellchecker for Dyslexia (ACM)](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/2700648.2809850); [PDF](https://www.superarladislexia.org/pdf/2015-Luz%20Rello-Spellchecker-assets.pdf)
- The same paper states that "people with dyslexia do not consciously detect spelling errors" (citing Rello's DysWebxia thesis, 2014), so their texts contain more errors even when they check. **[Limited; adults]** — [Rello et al. 2015 PDF](https://www.superarladislexia.org/pdf/2015-Luz%20Rello-Spellchecker-assets.pdf)
- Pedler's 2001 comparison of popular spellcheckers on dyslexic text found real-word errors were the error type *least* flagged. **[Moderate; adults/students; pre-2010]** — [Pedler 2001, British Journal of Educational Technology](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/229812535_Computer_spellcheckers_and_dyslexics-a_performance_survey)
- Pedler's best real-word method (semantic, using WordNet), tested on student-written dyslexic corpora, reached only 31.1% and 23.4% recall at 77 to 83% precision. Most real-word errors stayed undetected even by a specialised method of that era. **[Moderate; pre-2010]** — [Pedler 2007 PhD thesis](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/277288407_Computer_correction_of_real-word_spelling_errors_in_dyslexic_text) (figures as reported in [Rello et al. 2015](https://www.superarladislexia.org/pdf/2015-Luz%20Rello-Spellchecker-assets.pdf))
- Among mainstream tools in 2015, only Google Docs was competitive. It was tuned for high precision and lower recall, and detected about half of real-word errors. Real Check detected more. Real Check shows at most seven suggestions per word, filtered by frequency, a language model and grammar rules. **[Moderate; Spanish]** — [Rello et al. 2015 PDF](https://www.superarladislexia.org/pdf/2015-Luz%20Rello-Spellchecker-assets.pdf)
- Real Check user study (34 adults: 17 with dyslexia, 17 strong readers; 37 sentences each): **Dyslexic participants:** mean correction accuracy was 78.05 with no help, 89.83 with *error highlighting only* and 93.01 with *highlighting plus suggestions* (all significant). Suggestions also gave the shortest correction time. **Strong readers:** there was no significant accuracy effect. Dyslexic participants also *felt* more accurate with suggestions. Simply highlighting a suspect word gave dyslexic users most of the benefit. **[Moderate; adults; Spanish; corrections of given sentences, not free writing]** — [Rello et al. 2015 PDF](https://www.superarladislexia.org/pdf/2015-Luz%20Rello-Spellchecker-assets.pdf)
- In a Journal of Writing Research study of college students, real-word errors *rose* when spellcheck was active among students with dyslexia, a result the authors did not expect. This plausibly happens because accepting a wrong suggestion turns a non-word into a real-word error. **[Limited; adults; snippet only, mechanism not confirmed]** — [Use of Spellcheck in Text Production by College Students (JoWR)](https://www.jowr.org/index.php/jowr/article/download/580/465)
- Mossige et al. (2023, *Dyslexia*) analysed spellcheckers, word prediction, auto-correction, TTS and STT in nine widely used Nordic programs. Students with *moderate* spelling difficulties can now reach accurate spelling with the most sophisticated aids. However, most features "require time and attention", which can disturb writing fluency and hamper text production. The conflict between spelling accuracy and fluency "must be actively managed", and accuracy for *severe* spellers remains unclear. **[Limited; students; feature analysis; abstract]** — [Mossige et al. 2023, Dyslexia](https://doi.org/10.1002/dys.1752)
- Ten of the 19 LaMPost adults relied on a trusted third-party spell checker "such as Grammarly" over their email platform's built-in checker, specifically to catch real-word errors ("there" and "their"). **[Limited; adults]** — [Goodman et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2207.02308)
- Wu et al. (Meta, CHI 2019) built a neural machine translation model that converts "dyslexia-style" writing into standard writing for social media posts. In a one-week field study, 19 people with dyslexia gave highly positive feedback. **[Limited; adults; abstract]** — [Wu et al. 2019, Design and Evaluation of a Social Media Writing Support Tool for People with Dyslexia](https://ai.meta.com/research/publications/design-and-evaluation-of-a-social-media-writing-support-tool-for-people-with-dyslexia/)
- Ghotit (dyslexia-specific checker) features, as described in listings: a context-based spell checker "tuned for people with dyslexia", correction of confused words and homophones, suggestions shown *with meanings* from an integrated dictionary, "speak as you write" speech feedback for letters, words and sentences, and text-to-speech with dual highlighting. **[Vendor]** — [Ghotit Real Writer (U. Michigan Dyslexia Help)](https://dyslexiahelp.umich.edu/apps/ghotit-real-writer/); [Ghotit on Microsoft Store](https://apps.microsoft.com/detail/9n91n2jqgbzv)
- The Ghotit iPad keyboard offers talking word prediction and voice feedback "for users who learn better phonetically". Text correction needs an internet connection, and other features work offline. **[Vendor]** — [Ghotit iPad Dyslexia Keyboard (U. Michigan)](https://dyslexiahelp.umich.edu/latest/ghotit-ipad-dyslexia-keyboard/)
- NLP research continues on "confusion set" (homophone and near-homophone) disambiguation specifically as an aid for dyslexic writers. **[Limited]** — [Disambiguating Confusion Sets as an Aid for Dyslexic Spelling, READI 2020](https://aclanthology.org/2020.readi-1.1.pdf)

### Inferences
- Lexi's current "✨ Tidy spelling" (whole-text corrected version, accept or reject) suits a journal. For dyslexic users it should (a) cover real-word and homophone errors, which is where generic checkers fail and where LLM-based correction plausibly does better, (b) show changes word by word rather than as a whole rewritten block, so users can see what changed, and (c) let users hear each suggestion read aloud, and ideally see a meaning cue. Lexi's emoji and word descriptors could serve as visual meaning cues for homophones (e.g., "hear 👂 / here 📍"). The Ghotit "meanings with suggestions" pattern supports this.
- Rello's result that *highlight-only* already gave large gains suggests a gentle "Lexi isn't sure about these words" mode for *optional* review. It should never run during drafting, in line with Mossige's accuracy-versus-fluency conflict.
- The JoWR finding (spellcheck raising real-word errors) is a reason to avoid auto-correct and one-tap "accept all" for low-confidence corrections, and to keep the user's original text one tap away.

### Gaps
- I found no independent, published evaluation of **Grammarly** or **Texthelp Read&Write** accuracy on dyslexic or phonetic misspellings. Claims about these tools rest on vendor material, which I did not retrieve, plus user reports such as LaMPost's.
- I found no peer-reviewed evaluation of **Ghotit's** accuracy. Its features are vendor-described.
- I found no published accuracy benchmark of LLM-based correction (GPT- or Claude-class) on authentic dyslexic text corpora. LaMPost's authors note that no public corpus of adult dyslexic writing existed (as of 2022).
- The Rello study is Spanish and used sentence-correction tasks. Transfer to English free journaling is assumed, not shown.
- I found no evidence on "confidence display" UI (showing how sure the checker is) for dyslexic users specifically.

## 3. Word prediction / autocomplete, including phonetic prediction

### Takeaway
Word prediction with phonetic or "inventive" spelling support improves spelling accuracy and legibility for struggling writers. The evidence comes mostly from small single-subject studies of children, or of students with physical disabilities. Gains in the *amount* written are inconsistent, and prediction adds scanning and selection effort. Adults with dyslexia report liking modern predictive text (e.g., Smart Compose) for word-finding.

### Cited Findings
- Evmenova et al. (2010, *LD Research & Practice*) compared word prediction programs with phonetic spelling support (Co:Writer, WordQ, WriteAssist) during **journal writing**. Six students in grades 3 to 6 with severe writing or spelling difficulties took part. The study found evidence for word prediction over plain word processing and improved spelling accuracy in all prediction conditions. **[Moderate; CHILD-ONLY; single-subject, n=6]** — [Evmenova, Graff, Jerome & Behrmann 2010 (ERIC)](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ974602); [author PDF](https://mason.gmu.edu/~aevmenov/Portfolio/Growth/Word_prediction.pdf)
- Earlier MacArthur work, as cited by Evmenova, found that readability or legibility and spelling of students with LD improve with word prediction. **[Moderate; children; pre-2010; secondary citation]** — [Evmenova et al. 2010 (SAGE abstract)](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1111/j.1540-5826.2010.00315.x)
- With 24 students with physical disabilities, word processing and/or Co:Writer produced higher percentages of legible words, correctly spelled words and correct word sequences than handwriting, but **no difference in total words** written in 10 minutes. **[Moderate; children/teens; not dyslexia; pre-2010]** — [Mirenda et al. 2006 (ERIC)](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ767710)
- Co:Writer 4000 improved typing rate and spelling accuracy for *some* of three students with physical disabilities. Effectiveness depended on the individual. **[Very limited; not dyslexia; pre-2010]** — [Mezei & Heller 2005 (ERIC)](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ842009)
- A WordQ review argues prediction is especially beneficial for learners with dyslexia and ESL learners. It is a vendor-adjacent overview, not a controlled trial. **[Vendor/Limited]** — [The Effectiveness of Word Prediction Software WordQ (ERIC)](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1240594.pdf)
- Nine of the 19 LaMPost adults named predictive text (e.g., Gmail Smart Compose) as particularly helpful for finding their desired wording. Text suggestions can help with word-retrieval difficulties. **[Limited; adults]** — [Goodman et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2207.02308)
- Ghotit's "Quick-Spell" prediction is described as based on spelling, context, grammar and frequent or recent words. It is aimed at phonetic writers and at correcting the first misspelled letters as the user types. **[Vendor]** — [Ghotit (U. Michigan Dyslexia Help)](https://dyslexiahelp.umich.edu/apps/ghotit-real-writer/)
- Mossige et al. (2023) included word prediction among features that "require time and attention" and can disturb writing fluency. **[Limited; students]** — [Mossige et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1002/dys.1752)
- A registered clinical trial, "Spelling Mistake Tolerant Word Prediction for Working, Writing and Communicating", exists. I did not retrieve its results. **[Unknown]** — [ClinicalTrials.gov NCT04210661](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT04210661)

### Inferences
- In a feelings journal, the most valuable kind of prediction is probably **emotion vocabulary**: suggesting feeling words, possibly paired with Lexi's emoji descriptors, from a phonetic or misspelled start (e.g., "frus…" or "frustr8" → "frustrated 😤"). This combines phonetic tolerance, the Ghotit pattern, with Lexi's emoji-first design. It is an inference and has not been tested.
- Because prediction costs attention, it should be opt-in, show few options (the LaMPost finding below suggests 3 to 5 at most), read options aloud on long-press, and stay out of the way during free-writing.

### Gaps
- I found no study of word prediction specifically with dyslexic *adults* or teens, and no study of LLM-era phonetic prediction for dyslexia.
- I found no evidence comparing emoji or picture-supported prediction with text-only prediction.

## 4. AI rewriting and grammar help: autonomy, voice, control; what dyslexic users say

### Takeaway
The best dyslexia-specific evidence, Google's LaMPost study with 19 adults, shows strong interest in AI rewriting. It was the top-rated feature. Users felt in control *only when they could choose among options and make the final decision*. Main problems were hallucinated details, rewrites that ignored instructions, too many choices, opacity and privacy concerns. Surveys of disabled students (2025) show heavy use of ChatGPT and rewriting apps, plus worries about accuracy, integrity, cost and skill dependence. I found no 2023 to 2026 survey from Made By Dyslexia, BDA, Understood or Microsoft that I could verify.

### Cited Findings
**LaMPost (Goodman et al., ASSETS 2022; CACM 2024): adults with dyslexia**
- Design and sample: an LLM-powered email editor with three features: "Identify Main Ideas" (outline plus subject line), "Rewrite My Selection" (free-form instructions) and "Suggest Possible Changes". A formative study with seven adults came first, then a remote evaluation with 19 adults (32 recruited). Partners included the British Dyslexia Association and Understood.org. **[Limited; adults]** — [Goodman et al. 2022 (arXiv)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2207.02308); [ACM ASSETS](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3517428.3544819)
- In the formative study, adults wanted help with clarity, verbosity, tone and ordering ideas. They worried about their ability to act on AI feedback, about "maintaining autonomy and control over their work", and about privacy. **[Limited; adults]** — [Goodman et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2207.02308)
- Usefulness ratings on a 7-point scale: Rewrite My Selection 5.26 (13 of 19 agreed it was useful, 9 chose it as most useful), Main Ideas 5.11 and Suggest Changes 5.05. The Suggest ratings were polarised, with six users rating it most useful and six least useful. **[Limited; adults]** — [Goodman et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2207.02308)
- Accuracy problems: rewrites added "seemingly relevant yet imaginary details" (hallucinations), and four participants got rewrites that did not follow their instruction. The authors concluded that early-2022 LLMs "may not surpass the accuracy and quality thresholds" dyslexic writers need. **[Limited; adults; note that model quality has improved since]** — [Goodman et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2207.02308); [CACM 2024 version](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3626952)
- Choice overload: 12 participants worried about the sheer volume of options ("paradox of choice"). One said that spiralling through choices could lead to "more self-doubt". Most wanted about **3 to 5 options**, with nothing to scroll. Suggestions included sorting by length and replacing a dismissed option with a new one. **[Limited; adults]** — [Goodman et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2207.02308)
- Control: the mean "felt in control" rating was 5.58 out of 7. Feeling in control was tied to filtering results and making the final decision ("in the end, I'm the one that's deciding what it's going to say"). The three dissenters cited a subject line generated without choices, the opaque source of suggestions, and no fine-grained control once a process started. **[Limited; adults]** — [Goodman et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2207.02308)
- Privacy and trust: seven of 19 raised concerns about the system reading and storing personal data, and users asked for a clear data policy. This is highly relevant to a journal about feelings. **[Limited; adults]** — [Goodman et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2207.02308)
- Personalisation: six participants wanted the AI to learn *their* diction and tone from past writing. The authors note this conflicts with privacy. **[Limited; adults]** — [Goodman et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2207.02308)
- Writing instructions to the AI was itself hard. Some users struggled to find words for the change they wanted, or misspelled their instructions. The authors suggest pre-written instructions and a dictation option. They also suggest "?" explanation buttons and feedback flags for bad output. **[Limited; adults]** — [Goodman et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2207.02308)
- Telling users "this is AI" (AI-metaphor framing) had no measurable effect on perceived autonomy, expression or self-efficacy. **[Limited; adults]** — [Goodman et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2207.02308)

**Other AI and autonomy evidence**
- Wu et al. (CHI 2019) found that people with dyslexia worry their mistakes remain visible in a post's *edit history*, which undermines their efforts to present a positive self-image. **[Limited; adults]** — [Wu et al. 2019 (Meta AI)](https://ai.meta.com/research/publications/design-and-evaluation-of-a-social-media-writing-support-tool-for-people-with-dyslexia/)
- A 2025 survey of students with disabilities in higher education (124 valid responses) found dyslexia among the main conditions affecting writing. ChatGPT and rewriting apps were the most-used tools. Key concerns were inaccurate AI answers, academic integrity and subscription costs. **[Limited; adults; snippet only]** — [The use of generative AI by students with disabilities in higher education (ScienceDirect, 2025)](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1096751625000235)
- A 2025 review of studies on neurodivergent students (ADHD, dyslexia, dyspraxia, autism) reports ChatGPT use for summarising and essay structuring, and concerns about skill erosion and dependency. In one example, a dyslexic student questioned whether people still need to learn spelling when AI corrects it automatically. **[Limited; adults; secondary, snippet]** — [Human-AI Interactions: Cognitive, Behavioral, and Emotional Impacts (arXiv 2510.17753)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2510.17753)

### Inferences
- For a feelings journal, the stakes for "voice" are higher than for email. The text is *for the self*, so polish has little value and authenticity has a lot. This argues for: (1) a default of **no rewriting**, with Tidy limited to spelling and homophones and never style or tone; (2) per-word or per-change accept, with the original always kept and restorable; (3) at most about three alternatives when any are offered; (4) never adding content (hallucination risk). The original entry should be the saved version unless the user actively chooses otherwise.
- LaMPost's privacy findings mean that any cloud AI on journal text needs explicit, plain-language consent (read aloud), a clear statement of what is sent and stored, and preferably a per-entry opt-in.
- Wu's edit-history finding implies that Lexi should never expose "before and after" spelling to anyone else, for example in shared or exported entries, unless the user chooses.
- Pre-written, tappable Tidy options ("just fix spelling", "fix the words I'm not sure of", "read it to me") fit LaMPost's recommendation to scaffold instructions instead of requiring typed prompts.

### Gaps
- **I found no verifiable 2023 to 2026 survey from Made By Dyslexia, the British Dyslexia Association, Understood.org, or Microsoft Research** on dyslexic people's attitudes to generative AI writing tools. Searches returned only LaMPost, opinion pieces and general disability-student surveys. These sources may exist but were not surfaced. The report writer should not cite them without verification.
- I found no study of AI *spelling-only* correction (as opposed to rewriting) in a personal or emotional writing context.
- I found no evidence on whether AI correction over time affects dyslexic adults' spelling confidence or skill, positively or negatively.

## 5. Alternatives to writing for journaling: voice, photo/drawing, sentence starters, structured prompts, emoji

### Takeaway
Direct evidence that non-text journaling modes help *dyslexic* journalers specifically is essentially absent. Indirect evidence supports speech as an output channel: it removes the typing and spelling disadvantage, adults say talking is easier than writing, and voice journaling is an emerging research area. Scaffolds such as outlines or bullets, and pre-written prompts, are what dyslexic adults themselves use and request.

### Cited Findings
- Adults with dyslexia said verbal communication is easier than writing, and six of 19 already dictated their thoughts. Others drafted with bullet points (n=3) or the "word faucet" (n=5). Four wished the AI could expand their bullet outline into text. **[Limited; adults]** — [Goodman et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2207.02308)
- When learners with dyslexia answered by speech instead of typing, their performance gap with typical learners disappeared. This is not a journaling study, but it shows the output-modality effect. **[Moderate; learners]** — [Wilschut et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1111/tops.12769)
- In the Nordic STT intervention, removing the spelling barrier allowed growth in vocabulary diversity and overall text quality, and producing visibly longer texts may motivate continued use. **[Moderate; young teens]** — [Almgren Bäck et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1080/17483107.2024.2351488)
- Expressive-writing participants (breast cancer survivors, not dyslexic) described writing as "The Page That Does Not Judge", a private space for fear, grief and anger. This is relevant to framing a private journal as non-judgmental. **[Limited; adults; not dyslexia]** — [Ali & Shaban 2026, Seminars in Oncology Nursing](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.soncn.2026.152394)
- A 2026 study titled "Digital Voice Journaling for Depressive Symptoms in Youth: Predicting Symptom Changes Using Machine Learning" (Ku et al., *Healthcare*) shows voice journaling is an active research area. I could not retrieve its abstract. **[Unknown]** — [Europe PMC search for the title](https://europepmc.org/search?query=%22Digital%20Voice%20Journaling%22)

### Inferences
- A "voice entry" mode (audio saved, transcript optional and editable, transcript errors forgiven because the audio is the record) is the alternative best supported by the indirect evidence for dyslexic users. It also fits Lexi's existing native dictation.
- Sentence starters and structured prompts map onto the bullet-point and outline strategies dyslexic adults already use. Lexi's guided emoji check-ins already play this role, and starters could be read aloud and tappable.
- Emoji-, sticker- and photo-based entries lower the writing burden to near zero. Their therapeutic value compared with written expression is unproven (see Gaps). They are best framed as a valid entry in their own right, with optional words added.

### Gaps
- **I found no studies on voice journaling, photo or drawing journaling, or emoji or sticker entries for dyslexic people**, and none comparing their wellbeing effects with written journaling.
- I did not verify the classic expressive-disclosure literature comparing *talking* (e.g., into a recorder) with *writing* about emotions. Studies from the 1990s (e.g., Esterling et al., and Murray & Segal) are commonly cited as showing comparable benefits for vocal disclosure, but this pass did not retrieve or confirm them. A follow-up search is needed before the report claims voice entries are as therapeutic as written ones.
- I found no evidence on sentence starters or prompt scaffolds specifically for dyslexic adults' personal writing.

## 6. Fear of writing / writing anxiety in dyslexic adults and anxiety-reducing UI

### Takeaway
Dyslexic people report lasting dissatisfaction with and self-consciousness about their writing ("I'm never happy with what I write"). Spelling-specific worry and rumination start early. Adults use avoidance strategies, ask others to proofread, and fear visible mistakes. I found no controlled study testing UI choices such as hiding red squiggles or "spelling doesn't matter" framing. The design case rests on (a) the fluency cost of checking features, (b) the self-presentation concerns, and (c) privacy and control findings.

### Cited Findings
- Reynolds & Wu (2018) interviewed 11 people with dyslexia about reading and writing on Facebook. The paper's title quote, "I'm Never Happy with What I Write", captures persistent dissatisfaction and self-consciousness, and the paper describes challenges and coping strategies for social self-presentation. **[Limited; adults; abstract/title]** — [Reynolds & Wu 2018, AAAI ICWSM](https://aaai.org/papers/00280-15004-im-never-happy-with-what-i-write-challenges-and-strategies-of-people-with-dyslexia-on-social-media/)
- People with dyslexia worry that their mistakes stay visible in edit history, undermining their efforts to present a positive self-image. **[Limited; adults]** — [Wu et al. 2019](https://ai.meta.com/research/publications/design-and-evaluation-of-a-social-media-writing-support-tool-for-people-with-dyslexia/)
- LaMPost participants described writing drafts elsewhere, on pen and paper or another platform, partly "to separate it from the anxiety of having to respond" (P2). Others described the frustration of "putting what I'm trying to say in my brain into words", time pressure that kept them from revising (n=7), and misunderstandings of their tone (n=6) or clarity (n=7). **[Limited; adults]** — [Goodman et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2207.02308)
- In a large Dutch sample (90 students with dyslexia compared with 1,411 without), students with dyslexia reported lower spelling self-concept and less enjoyment of spelling, and more *worrying and rumination* and support-seeking about spelling. For reading they reported more anxiety and shame. **[Moderate; CHILD-ONLY, primary school; abstract]** — [Dams et al. 2026, Dyslexia](https://doi.org/10.1002/dys.70034)
- A PLOS ONE paper cites another study finding that 48% of participants with dyslexia received significantly more negative peer feedback on social media posts, compared with about 22% of controls. **[Very limited; secondary citation via snippet, original not checked]** — [PLOS ONE 2023, internet-related addictions among adults with dyslexia](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0280555)
- Checking features (spellcheck, prediction, auto-correct) "require time and attention" and can disturb writing fluency. This supports separating drafting from checking. **[Limited; students]** — [Mossige et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1002/dys.1752)
- Highlighting suspect words *did* improve dyslexic users' correction accuracy when they were explicitly correcting. Highlighting is therefore not harmful in itself. The question is *when* to show it. **[Moderate; adults]** — [Rello et al. 2015](https://www.superarladislexia.org/pdf/2015-Luz%20Rello-Spellchecker-assets.pdf)
- Context: a Texthelp-commissioned Censuswide survey of more than 2,000 UK adults found over a third struggled with literacy at school, and grammar was the topic found most challenging. **[Vendor-commissioned survey; general population, not dyslexia-specific]** — [Texthelp press release](https://website-us.texthelp.com/about/press-release/over-a-third-of-uk-adults-struggled-with-literacy-at-school)

### Inferences
- Lexi's "Spelling doesn't matter here" framing and the absence of red squiggles while drafting fit the evidence that checking interrupts flow and that visible mistakes feel exposing. Spelling marks are best shown *only* in an explicit, user-initiated review ("✨ Tidy"), using neutral styling (soft underline or dotted line, not red) and neutral wording ("Lexi isn't sure about...", not "error").
- Private-by-default drafts, no visible correction history, and the original text kept as the canonical version respond directly to the self-presentation and edit-history concerns.
- Avoiding time pressure (no timers, autosave, "come back later") addresses the reported lack of time to revise.
- Offering read-aloud of the user's own entry lets them check meaning without visual proofreading, the strategy adults already use.

### Gaps
- **I found no controlled or user study testing "no red squiggles", "no wrong spelling" framing, or private-draft UIs** on dyslexic adults' writing anxiety or output.
- I found no validated measure of writing anxiety used specifically with dyslexic adults in an app context, and no adult (as opposed to child) quantitative data on spelling-related anxiety from this search.
- I did not retrieve the full Reynolds & Wu (2018) text, so specific strategy frequencies (avoidance, proofreading by others) are not quantified here.
