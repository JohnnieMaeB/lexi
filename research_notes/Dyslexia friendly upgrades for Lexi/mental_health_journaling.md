# Mental-health and journaling context for Lexi (dyslexia-friendly emoji-first journaling chatbot) and competitor features worth borrowing

Researcher notes, compiled 2026-10-17. Evidence tags used below: **[Strong]** = peer-reviewed meta-analysis/large review read at abstract or full-text level; **[Moderate]** = single peer-reviewed study/review or official guidance; **[Weak]** = secondary summary, preprint, thesis, news; **[Vendor]** = company's own claim (not independently verified); **[Practitioner]** = blog/opinion.
Two premise corrections: (1) Wilmot et al. 2023 is a *scoping review* (98 studies), not a meta-analysis, and it reports no pooled prevalence; (2) Mugnaini et al. 2009 is a *narrative review* of published studies, not a primary prevalence study. The nearest actual meta-analysis I found is Vieira et al. 2024 (see Q1).
Retrieval caveats: PubMed and PMC pages were blocked to the fetch tool; abstracts were obtained via Europe PMC or search summaries where noted. Jurisdiction is flagged on every legal or regulatory item.

## Q1. Dyslexia and mental health: prevalence, recurring emotional themes, strengths-based framings

### Takeaway
Dyslexic and other learning-difficulty groups show moderately elevated internalizing problems (anxiety, depression, low self-concept) versus controls (pooled g about -0.54 across 96 studies), with the best evidence suggesting reading difficulties precede the emotional difficulties. Recurring qualitative themes are school-based failure/humiliation, late or missed identification ("something is wrong with me"), stigma-driven concealment, and identity conflict; strengths-based framings (Made By Dyslexia) are popular but rest on advocacy sources, and academics caution that compensatory strengths are not universal.

### Cited Findings
- Vieira et al. (Annals of Dyslexia, 2024; special issue on dyslexia and mental health): systematic review and meta-analysis of internalizing problems in reading, maths and unspecified learning difficulties. 96 studies, 120 unique samples, 83,260 participants, ages about 7.3-34.8. Random-effects Hedges' g = -0.54 (moderate): affected groups report more internalizing problems than age-matched controls. Learning-difficulty type and age did NOT moderate the effect (reading difficulties did not differ from maths/unspecified); selection method (diagnosis vs screening) and type of internalizing problem did. Searched studies 2000-April 2023, English-language. [Strong] — [ACU research bank record](https://acuresearchbank.acu.edu.au/items/04478e26-b53f-4b0c-9fa8-ab8c0ac4bfe2); [Annals of Dyslexia 2024 issue listing](https://www.springermedicine.com/annals-of-dyslexia-1-2024/26707104). Note: pooled sample is mostly children/adolescents and mixes dyslexia with other learning difficulties; the abstract does not give separate anxiety vs depression effect sizes (I did not read the full text).
- Wilmot, Hasking, Leitao, Hill & Boyes (2023), International Journal of Environmental Research and Public Health, scoping review: 98 studies (86 journal articles, 12 theses) from 20 countries on childhood dyslexia/reading difficulty and internalising/externalising problems. Opens with "Children with dyslexia are at elevated risk of internalising and externalising mental health concerns"; anxiety flagged as a particular concern; not every study found group differences; where differences exist, the balance of evidence suggests reading difficulties precede internalising symptoms (direction less certain for externalising). [Moderate] — [PMC9864451 (text read via Europe PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9864451); [MDPI version](https://www.mdpi.com/1660-4601/20/2/1653)
- Same review, "third factors": self-esteem/self-concept/self-efficacy (30 studies; low scholastic self-esteem associated with internalising symptoms, mostly cross-sectional, mixed across domains); stress/coping/resilience (18 studies; grit and resilience linked to less anxiety and depression; hypervigilance about school problems linked to internalising symptoms); school connectedness (2 studies, possibly a key protective factor; children who felt their teacher understood their learning disability reported lower anxiety); stigma/discrimination (only qualitative studies, 3; participants described stigma, shame and discrimination in learning environments); bullying consistently linked to mental-health concerns; emotion regulation under-studied. Gaps: primary-school focus, heavy reliance on parent/teacher report, few secondary-school or adult samples. [Moderate] — [PMC9864451](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9864451)
- Same review cites a general estimate that 5-10% of children have severe, persistent word-reading difficulties consistent with dyslexia (not a pooled mental-health prevalence). [Moderate] — [PMC9864451](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9864451)
- Mugnaini, Lassi, La Malfa & Albertini (2009), World Journal of Pediatrics 5(4):255-264, "Internalizing correlates of dyslexia" — a literature review (PubMed, PsycArticles, Academic Search Elite). Concludes dyslexia is a specific risk factor for increased internalizing, anxious and depressive symptoms; dyslexia severity, comorbid ADHD, lower perceived social support and female gender most affect psychosocial outcomes. The abstract gives no numeric prevalence or effect sizes. [Moderate/dated] — [Europe PMC record](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=%22Internalizing%20correlates%20of%20dyslexia%22&format=json&resultType=core); DOI 10.1007/s12519-009-0049-7.
- Hamilton Clark (2024), Journal of Research in Special Educational Needs (UK; DOI 10.1111/1471-3802.12683): semi-structured interviews with 5 dyslexic university students plus learning-support tutors at 4 UK universities. Concealing dyslexia led to identity conflict and low self-esteem, compounding low academic self-concept, "particularly when dyslexia had been unrecognised through school." A tutor estimated 75-80% of dyslexic students who come through self-referral screening did not know they were dyslexic and often say "I always knew there was something wrong with me." Participants described humiliation at being held back, truancy misread as misbehaviour, absorbed self-doubt ("do I even really deserve to be here?"), one participant in therapy for low self-value, and derogatory lecturer comments ("reading retardation") that "increased the shame." Tutors said one negative remark "can override all the positive." The paper states it supports prior links between dyslexia and low self-esteem, anxiety and depression (Burden 2008) and notes the mask-through-coping-strategies observation (van Viersen et al. 2015). Small qualitative sample, university-only, UK. [Moderate-Weak] — [UCL Discovery PDF](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10192362/1/Hamilton%20Clark_Dyslexia%20concealment.pdf)
- Other qualitative leads (abstract/summary level only, not opened): Speers (2019) auto/biographical narrative thesis, 6 adults, themes include disclosure and concealment and identity anxiety [Weak] — [Canterbury repository](https://repository.canterbury.ac.uk/item/8vqy3/-an-auto-biographical-investigation-exploring-the-life-stories-of-adults-with-dyslexia). A 17-adult study found school narratives marked by failure, with emotions softening in adulthood though impact on the present remained [Weak] (as summarised in search results; see [Sandman-Hurley, Perspectives on Language](https://www.onlinedigeditions.com/article/Dyslexia%3A+Treating+the+Trauma+Before+the+Symptoms/4707239/812637/article.html), which describes adults recalling classrooms with feelings from sadness to rage to apathy, strongest where support was absent). A New Zealand IPA study of 8 adults in primary industries used a strengths-based approach; themes: constructing/maintaining identity, connectedness to the world, "the conundrum of time" [Weak] — [Otago archive](https://ourarchive.otago.ac.nz/handle/10523/7906).
- Strengths framing: Made By Dyslexia (with EY) published "Value of Dyslexia" reports in 2018 and 2019 positing dyslexic strengths such as problem-solving, creativity, lateral thinking, pattern recognition, spatial reasoning, interpersonal communication; matches to World Economic Forum "skills of the future"; LinkedIn reportedly added "dyslexic thinking" as a skill. Coverage is advocacy/marketing; I could not independently verify the EY figures. [Weak/advocacy] — [Made By Dyslexia resources](https://madebydyslexia.org/?p=4323); [Microsoft Education blog](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/education/blog/?p=6825); [Happiful](https://happiful.com/the-power-of-dyslexic-thinking).
- Counterweight to universal strengths narratives: Hamilton Clark cites van Viersen et al. (2015) suggesting higher-ability dyslexic students do not show consistent compensatory strengths but mostly efficiently mask impact through study methods and coping strategies; dyslexia is independent of intelligence, and "lazy or slow" is a common misconception. [Moderate-Weak] — [UCL Discovery PDF](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10192362/1/Hamilton%20Clark_Dyslexia%20concealment.pdf)
- Leads not opened: a narrative review of positive-psychology interventions to raise self-esteem/self-efficacy/confidence and lower anxiety in dyslexic students ([MDPI 2025](https://www.mdpi.com/2673-995X/4/2/55)); a follow-up on dyslexia, school connectedness, depression and anxiety across the primary-secondary transition ([PMC13360781](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13360781/)).

### Inferences
- Lexi's users will include many people for whom writing itself has been a site of school shame; a writing-heavy flow (word counts, long free-text) risks re-triggering that history. The emoji-first/low-text design is well aligned with the "shame/concealment" theme.
- Because unrecognised dyslexia and "I always knew something was wrong with me" are common among adults, onboarding that normalises (without diagnosing or assuming a diagnosis) is safer than "you have dyslexia" framing; support can't assume all users are diagnosed.
- Strengths framing is best offered as optional content (e.g., in a "reflect on what went well your way" prompt) rather than a mandatory positive spin, given the toxic-positivity literature (Q3) and the masking evidence that not everyone experiences compensatory strengths.
- Evidence is largely child/adolescent and cross-sectional; adult-specific claims in Lexi's marketing should be hedged ("research links dyslexia with higher rates of anxiety and low self-esteem") rather than quantified.

### Gaps
- No adult-specific pooled prevalence of anxiety/depression in dyslexia retrieved (Klassen, Tze & Hannok 2013 adult learning-disability meta-analysis appeared in search results but I did not retrieve its findings; search snippets about a 14-vs-14 Italian student study and a population twin study on anxiety disorders were unattributed, so excluded).
- Vieira full-text breakdown by anxiety vs depression vs self-esteem and by dyslexia-only samples not read.
- No source retrieved on self-harm/suicidality rates in dyslexia (relevant to crisis design).
- Made By Dyslexia/EY primary reports were not accessed.
- No research found specifically on "masking" as an emotional-labour construct in dyslexic adults beyond university students.

## Q2. Expressive writing and journaling evidence; transfer to spoken/recorded, drawing, structured prompts; dyslexic or low-literacy participants

### Takeaway
Expressive writing has a small but reliable average benefit in healthy and subclinical samples (Frattaroli 2006 r = .075; Guo 2023 g = -0.12), with mixed results in clinical or low-expressivity groups, and benefits larger when sessions are closely spaced. Evidence for spoken/recorded disclosure is thin and mixed (one RCT in rheumatoid arthritis found different, not equivalent, benefits), and I found NO studies of expressive writing or speech-based disclosure in dyslexic or low-literacy participants; drawing/art was not covered.

### Cited Findings
- Frattaroli (2006, Psychological Bulletin): meta-analysis of 146 randomized experimental-disclosure studies; random-effects average r = .075 (small but significant benefit); several moderators identified. [Strong, dated] — [Frattaroli 2006 PDF](https://Www.Gwern.net/doc/psychology/2006-frattaroli.pdf)
- Summary of later work: some meta-analyses report benefit (Frattaroli 2006; Frisina 2004; Pavlacic 2019; Travagin 2015) while others found no improvement in psychological outcomes (Meads & Nouwen 2005; Reinhold 2018) — i.e. the literature is genuinely mixed. [Weak, secondary] — [Simply Psychology summary](https://www.simplypsychology.org/expressive-writing-for-anxiety.html)
- Guo (2023), British Journal of Clinical Psychology 62(1):272-297: 31 RCTs, N = 4,012, healthy/subclinical samples with at least one follow-up; overall Hedges' g = -0.12 (95% CI -0.21 to -0.04) for depression, anxiety and stress combined; stronger effects when sessions were 1-3 days apart (Gdiff = -0.18) than 4-7 or >7 days; number of sessions did not moderate. Stated limitations: only healthy/subclinical samples; "writing content and writing skill were not examined as moderators"; possible underpowered moderator analyses. Details taken from a summary, not the paper. [Strong for the paper, Weak for my access] — [Simply Psychology summary of Guo 2023](https://www.simplypsychology.org/expressive-writing-for-anxiety.html)
- Adolescents: a separate meta-analysis pooled 21 independent studies of expressive writing in 10-18-year-olds (effect size not retrieved). [Weak] — [NCJRS abstract](https://www.ojp.gov/index%2ephp/ncjrs/virtual-library/abstracts/how-effective-are-expressive-writing-interventions-adolescents-meta)
- Online/structured variants: Dean et al. (JMIR Mental Health 2016) RCT, 863 volunteers, online expressive writing vs directing people to an internet support group for depression/anxiety — all four outcomes (depression, anxiety, social support, life satisfaction) improved over 6 months with no difference between conditions [Moderate] — [JMIR Ment Health 2016;3(2):e12](https://mental.jmir.org/2016/2/e12/citations). Smyth et al. (JMIR Mental Health 2018) preliminary RCT of online *positive affect journaling* (a modified expressive-writing protocol) in medical patients with elevated anxiety [Moderate, preliminary] — [JMIR Ment Health 2018 PDF](https://mental.jmir.org/2018/4/e11290/PDF). A JMIR preprint (not peer-reviewed) of an 8-week mobile journaling platform RCT in 507 young adults with mild-to-moderate anxiety/depression reported modest anxiety reductions (d = 0.16-0.19) at 8 weeks and 1 month [Weak] — [JMIR preprint](https://preprints.jmir.org/preprint/102783).
- Moderators/harm signal: a UCLA randomized trial found no overall effect of expressive writing on anxiety or depression, with emotional expressiveness moderating outcome; authors suggested it may not suit people who don't typically express emotions. [Moderate] — [Niles et al., UCLA PDF](https://teams.semel.ucla.edu/sites/default/files/publications/Jan%202014%20-%20Randomized%20controlled%20trial%20of%20expressive%20writing%20for%20psychological%20and%20physical%20health.pdf)
- Spoken vs written disclosure: an RCT in adults with rheumatoid arthritis compared writing vs speaking into a recorder, each vs positive/neutral controls; both gave modest, inconsistent benefits that did not match — written disclosure improved pain at 1 and 6 months (and affective pain/walking speed in a later breakdown), spoken disclosure improved walking speed at 3 months and reduced pain, swollen joints and physician-rated disease activity at 6 months (attribution to Radcliffe and colleagues, Pain 2011 per search summary; PubMed fetch blocked, abstract not directly read). [Moderate, single trial, physical-health outcomes in a patient group] — [PubMed 21315515](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21315515/)
- Background on modality: early disclosure studies mostly used talking, and researchers later moved to writing largely for experimental control; only a few experiments have tested privately *talking* about stressful experiences, with mixed results (benefits in some, nulls in others). [Weak-Moderate; surfaced in the same search, source page not opened] — [PMC3065513](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3065513/)
- Learning-disability/dyslexia-adjacent: a 2009 VCU dissertation randomized students with disabilities to 15-minute typed expressive-writing sessions and found no significant reduction in stress or daily hassles vs control (authors cited possible ceiling effects) [Weak] — [VCU Scholars Compass](https://scholarscompass.vcu.edu/etd/1851). A 2014 chapter on dyslexia and expressive writing deals with writing difficulties and oral-language problems, not emotional disclosure [Weak, not relevant to effect] — [UCL Discovery](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/1541257/).

### Inferences
- The dyslexia-specific evidence base for expressive writing is effectively empty. Lexi should treat benefits as extrapolated from general populations and avoid clinical claims.
- Guo explicitly did not test writing skill as a moderator, so whether the mechanism depends on fluent text production is unknown; but the mechanism proposed for affect labeling (Q3) is label-based rather than composition-based, which makes an emoji + short phrase + optional voice approach plausible, not proven.
- Closely spaced short sessions (1-3 days apart) outperformed spaced ones in Guo; this supports gentle daily/near-daily check-ins over weekly long sessions, but also argues against pushing streak pressure (Q6).
- Because low-expressivity users may gain nothing (Niles), a structured check-in (mood + topics) may be a better default than open free writing, with free writing/voice available but not required. Structured/positive-affect variants have RCT support (Smyth) but at preliminary level.
- Voice journaling should be framed as "an easier way to get thoughts out", not as clinically equivalent to expressive writing.

### Gaps
- No studies found of expressive writing, journaling or voice/speech-to-text disclosure in dyslexic or low-literacy samples.
- Drawing/art-based expression and emoji-based expression: not researched; no evidence retrieved.
- Pennebaker's original protocol details (e.g., 15-20 minute sessions over 3-4 days) and Pennebaker 1997 primary text not verified in this pass (Pennebaker 1997 PDFs appeared in search but were not opened).
- Reinhold 2018, Pavlacic 2019, Travagin 2015 findings known only second-hand.
- Adverse effects/rumination risk of unguided journaling in depressed users not systematically reviewed.

## Q3. Mood tracking and emotion labeling: affect labeling, granularity, scales vs emoji vs words, toxic positivity, "neutral/content" moods

### Takeaway
Putting feelings into words reduces emotional reactivity in experimental work (affect labeling) and finer emotion differentiation is linked to better coping and fewer anxiety/depression symptoms, but the depression link is inconsistent and mechanisms are early-stage. Research on acceptance of negative emotion and the cost of pressured positivity supports Lexi's no-"Happy" choice, but I found no head-to-head evidence comparing emoji, numeric scales and word lists, and no source on how apps treat "neutral/content" moods.

### Cited Findings
- Torre & Lieberman (2018), Emotion Review 10(2):116-124, review: affect labeling (naming an emotion) acts as implicit emotion regulation, producing a pattern of effects similar to explicit regulation such as reappraisal across experiential, autonomic, neural and behavioural measures; earlier work found labels (vs non-emotional words) increased prefrontal inhibitory activity and reduced amygdala activation; candidate mechanisms (distraction, self-reflection, uncertainty reduction) and authors caution understanding is at an early stage. [Moderate; I read only abstract-level summaries] — [SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/1754073917742706); [Institute of Coaching summary](https://www.instituteofcoaching.org/node/36688)
- A GitHub-hosted, non-peer-reviewed, pre-registered meta-analysis of the Lieberman-paradigm (k = 9) reports g = -0.43 with a prediction interval crossing zero; authorship unverified. Do not cite as established. [Weak] — [repo listing](https://awesome.ecosyste.ms/projects/github.com%2Fmikhaeelatefrizk%2Faffect-labeling-review)
- Kashdan, Barrett & McKnight (2015), Current Directions in Psychological Science, "Unpacking emotion differentiation": people who distinguish negative emotions more finely tend to show fewer maladaptive coping behaviours and less severe anxiety and depressive disorders. [Moderate] — [APS page](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/journals/current-directions/0963721414550708/)
- Nuance on granularity and depression: a 2021 Frontiers in Psychology study proposed low differentiation as a stable feature of depression, but the association held on only one depression measure and did not survive adjustment for negative emotional intensity [Moderate] — [Frontiers 2021](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2021.685851/pdf); an adolescent study found a larger negative-emotion vocabulary was associated with MORE depressive symptoms while lower differentiation was independently linked as well, suggesting vocabulary size and differentiation are distinct [Weak, secondary summary] — [Simply Psychology](https://www.simplypsychology.org/?p=77896). A meta-analysis of complexity of emotion experience and behavioural adaptation (O'Toole et al. 2020) is cited in a 2022 Frontiers editorial but I did not retrieve its findings [Weak] — [PMC9714615](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9714615).
- Toxic positivity / acceptance: Bastian and colleagues report that placing a high value on happiness can lower happiness, especially where people most expect to feel happy, and that believing others expect you to be happy can leave people with negative feelings feeling socially disconnected; Mauss (UC Berkeley) found habitual acceptance of negative emotions was associated with fewer negative emotions and better psychological health. Findings are largely correlational and I saw them only in news/university coverage, not primary papers. [Weak-Moderate] — [Discover Magazine](https://www.discovermagazine.com/mind/toxic-positivity-can-leave-negative-emotions-unaddressed-and-impact-your); [Federation University](https://www.federation.edu.au/about/news/news/how-to-avoid-toxic-positivity-and-take-the-less-direct-route-to-happiness/); [Quartz on Mauss](https://qz.com/1052751/accepting-negative-emotions-leads-to-better-psychological-health)
- How We Feel (free, nonprofit, donation-funded iOS/Android app from Marc Brackett's Yale-linked RULER/Mood Meter work): Mood Meter now has 144 emotion words; users check in, tag what is affecting their mood, and get regulation-strategy videos/activities and pattern reports; App Store listing says data stays on device unless the user opts in to send anonymised check-ins for research (vendor statement). [Vendor/Moderate] — [Brackett site](https://marcbrackett.com/how-we-feel-app-3/); [App Store listing](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/-/id1562706384); [Yale Medicine](https://medicine.yale.edu/news-article/the-how-we-feel-app-helping-emotions-work-for-us-not-against-us/)
- Daylio uses a default mood scale with editable/custom emojis and the ability to add moods; Apple Journal logs "State of Mind" and saves it to Health. [Vendor] — [Daylio](https://daylio.net/); [Apple support](https://support.apple.com/guide/iphone/journal-for-your-wellbeing-iph7b79617d5/ios)

### Inferences
- Lexi's emoji-first mood grouping (Content / Stressed / Low self-esteem, no "Happy") is consistent with the acceptance and anti-pressured-positivity literature, but nobody has tested omitting a "happy" option; there is a risk that people feeling genuinely good don't see themselves in "Content". Worth user-testing a neutral/calm/okay and a "good day" option framed as non-pressured.
- Offering an optional second step from emoji to a specific word (granularity ladder, as in How We Feel's 144 words) fits the affect-labeling/differentiation evidence, but dyslexic users may find long word lists hard to read; pair words with emoji/colour and audio read-aloud (design hypothesis, not evidence from this research).
- The adolescent finding (bigger negative vocabulary linked to more depressive symptoms) suggests granularity should be offered as a gentle option, not a performance metric.

### Gaps
- No studies found comparing emoji vs numeric mood scales vs custom words for validity or for dyslexic/low-literacy users.
- No source found on how apps handle "neutral/content/okay" moods or on mood-scale valence balance.
- Primary Bastian/Humphrey and Mauss papers not opened; Lieberman 2007 original not read.
- No evidence on whether labeling via emoji (vs words) produces the same regulation effects.

## Q4. Safe AI companion / chatbot design for mental-health-adjacent apps; regulators and guidelines 2024-2026

### Takeaway
The APA's Nov 2025 health advisory (US) is the clearest professional guidance: disclose AI status persistently, never imply therapy or licensure, design against dependency and sycophancy, protect data, build crisis detection with psychologist input, and audit independently. US state law is moving fast (Illinois, Nevada bans on AI therapy; Utah disclosure/data rules; California SB 243 companion-chatbot safety protocols), the UK MHRA treats digital mental-health tools as devices depending on intended purpose, and Woebot's 2025 closure shows the regulatory cost of clinical claims.

### Cited Findings
**Professional guidance (US)**
- APA Health Advisory on the Use of Generative AI Chatbots and Wellness Applications for Mental Health (November 2025): most such tools are not designed for clinical feedback or treatment, may lack scientific validation and oversight, often lack adequate safety protocols and are not regulatory-approved; ability to manage a user in crisis is "limited and unpredictable"; not a substitute for a qualified provider but possibly an adjunct. Scope explicitly includes non-AI wellness apps such as symptom trackers, noting they are unregulated for safety/efficacy and not subject to health-care privacy laws. [Moderate/authoritative guidance] — [APA press release](https://www.apa.org/news/press/releases/2025/11/ai-wellness-apps-mental-health); [advisory PDF](https://www.infocop.es/wp-content/uploads/2025/11/health-advisory-ai-chatbots-wellness-apps-mental-health.pdf)
- Developer recommendations in the advisory: clear, prominent, *persistent* disclosure that the user is talking to an AI and that it cannot replace professional care; psychologists/subject-matter experts involved in safety features and guardrails; "nudges" encouraging breaks, limiting the AI's memory to avoid the illusion of a continuous relationship, reducing anthropomorphic features, and not persuading users away from real-life conversations; safeguards to detect and interrupt harmful conversations (e.g., self-harm, disordered eating) as "a clinical safety issue"; transparency on what data is collected, stored, shared, used for model training, and how users can permanently delete it; protocols for responding to disclosures of risk or maltreatment; never misrepresent as a licensed professional; independent third-party audits for safety, efficacy, bias and data security before release, plus ongoing human audit of a representative sample of interactions. [Moderate] — [advisory PDF](https://www.infocop.es/wp-content/uploads/2025/11/health-advisory-ai-chatbots-wellness-apps-mental-health.pdf)
- Dependency and sycophancy: the advisory warns many LLMs are trained to be agreeable/validating, which can reinforce cognitive distortions; warm responses, avatars and flattery amplify anthropomorphism; engagement-optimised design is likened to infinite scroll; it cites a statistic that 33% of teens would rather discuss something serious with an AI companion than a person; warns of false therapeutic alliance and of vulnerable users (children, those with mental-health histories) being harmed. Policy asks: safe-by-default privacy settings, no sale of AI-interaction health data, "mental privacy," prohibition on AI posing as licensed professionals. [Moderate] — [advisory PDF](https://www.infocop.es/wp-content/uploads/2025/11/health-advisory-ai-chatbots-wellness-apps-mental-health.pdf)
- The advisory classes some vulnerable users as relying on AI as their "only private or psychologically safe outlet," including where stigma or shame is present. [Moderate] — same PDF

**US state laws (jurisdiction: US states; verify statute text and effective dates before relying)**
- Illinois HB 1806 (Wellness and Oversight for Psychological Resources Act), signed 4 Aug 2025: bars AI from delivering psychotherapy, diagnostics, treatment plans, crisis intervention or therapeutic follow-up (per one law-firm summary — note "crisis intervention" wording to check against statute); admin uses allowed with consent; enforced by IDFPR; fines up to $10,000 per violation; terms such as "AI therapy," "chatbot counselor," "virtual psychotherapist" treated as misleading without clinician oversight. [Weak-Moderate; law-firm/news summaries, statute not read] — [Data Privacy & Security Insider](https://www.dataprivacyandsecurityinsider.com/2025/08/illinois-enacts-act-prohibiting-ai-therapy/); [Axios Chicago](https://www.axios.com/local/chicago/2025/08/06/illinois-ai-therapy-ban-mental-health-regulation); [Blueprint summary](https://blueprint.ai/blog/breaking-down-current-legislation-regulating-ai-in-mental-health-care)
- Nevada AB 406 (passed June 2025): AI systems may not provide mental or behavioural health care or claim they can; fines up to $15,000. Utah HB 452 (passed March 2025): mental-health chatbots must clearly disclose they are AI, may not sell or share user data, marketing restrictions. [Weak-Moderate, secondary] — [Blueprint summary](https://blueprint.ai/blog/breaking-down-current-legislation-regulating-ai-in-mental-health-care)
- California SB 243 (companion chatbots), signed 13 Oct 2025, effective 1 Jan 2026: "companion chatbot" = AI system with natural-language interface giving adaptive, human-like responses that can meet social needs and "sustain a relationship across multiple interactions"; exclusions include customer-service/business bots, game bots limited to the game, and stand-alone devices without sustained relationships (the Skadden summary does not say how wellness/journaling bots are treated). Operator duties: clear AI disclosure when a reasonable person might think it's human; protocol to prevent suicidal-ideation/self-harm content and refer users to crisis providers, published on the website; for known minors, disclosure plus a break reminder at least every 3 hours; annual reporting to the Office of Suicide Prevention from 1 July 2027 (no personal data); private right of action (greater of actual damages or $1,000 per violation, plus fees). [Moderate; law-firm summaries] — [Skadden](https://www.skadden.com/insights/publications/2025/10/new-california-companion-chatbot-law); [Future of Privacy Forum](https://fpf.org/blog/understanding-the-new-wave-of-chatbot-legislation-california-sb-243-and-beyond/). Conflict: the Blueprint page still lists SB 243 as "proposed" (outdated vs the Skadden/FPF reports of signing).
- Pending/other (New York S8484, New Jersey A5603, etc.): proposals to bar advertising AI as a licensed mental-health professional; status as of mid-2025 only. [Weak] — [Blueprint summary](https://blueprint.ai/blog/breaking-down-current-legislation-regulating-ai-in-mental-health-care)

**UK (jurisdiction: UK)**
- MHRA guidance on digital mental health technologies (DMHT), 3 Feb 2025, developed with NICE and NHS experts: whether a DMHT is Software as a Medical Device depends on intended purpose and functionality; Class I is low-risk and self-certified, Classes IIa-III need an Approved/Notified Body; manufacturers must meet UK device regulations and show the product is effective, reliable and acceptably safe. The press release does not mention mood tracking, journaling or generative AI. ### Inferences
- Lexi's current design (brief reflection, 988 on self-harm mention, per-user private data) matches several APA asks but likely lacks: persistent AI disclosure, a visible always-on help button (Wysa pattern), explicit "not therapy" language, memory limits/break nudges, user-controlled deletion/export, and any audit/testing record.
- 988 is a US service; non-US users (e.g., UK) need locale-appropriate resources. A locale picker or country-aware crisis resource list is needed if Lexi is distributed outside the US (jurisdictional inference; 988 details not sourced in this pass).
- Crisis detection must work for dyslexic writers: phonetic or misspelled self-harm phrases, and emoji-only entries, will defeat naive keyword filters (design hypothesis; no source tested this). Consider fuzzy/phonetic matching, LLM-based classification, and mood-pattern triggers rather than keywords alone.
- Avoid wording that could be read as "therapy," "counselor," or "crisis intervention" in the US-state context (Illinois, Nevada); position as a journaling/reflection tool. If Lexi sustains a relationship across sessions with memory it might fall under California SB 243's definition; seek legal review. If distributed in the UK, claims about diagnosing/treating would move it toward medical-device territory.
- Sycophancy guard: reflections should validate feelings without agreeing with distorted self-judgments about dyslexia ("I'm stupid") — a high-risk theme given Q1 findings.

## Q6. Reminders, streaks and gamification: engagement vs guilt/anxiety; gentle-nudge best practice

### Takeaway
Peer-reviewed consumer research shows visible intact streaks increase continued engagement, but broken streaks can cause drop-off, especially when users blame themselves, and "repairable" streaks reduce this damage. I found no controlled study of streak-induced anxiety or guilt in mental-health or journaling apps; guidance on gentle design comes mostly from practitioners and from the APA's anti-engagement-maximising stance.

### Cited Findings
- Silverman & Barasch (2023), Journal of Consumer Research 49(6):1095-1117, "On or Off Track: How (Broken) Streaks Affect Consumer Decisions": seven studies; displaying an intact streak (vs a broken one) in a behavioural log increased subsequent engagement independent of actual past behaviour, because people treat maintaining a logged streak as a goal in itself; the effect is amplified when people attribute a break to themselves, and attenuated when the streak can be "repaired." Barasch (interview) cautioned that messages highlighting a recent failure don't work and that people tend to drop off once streaks break; suggested offering a new goal instead. [Moderate-Strong; read via abstract and press summaries] — [CU Boulder](https://www.colorado.edu/business/faculty-research/2023/04/19/or-track-how-broken-streaks-affect-consumer-decisions); [UD repository record](https://udspace.udel.edu/handle/19716/34160)
- Summary of the same literature: users value extending a streak more than the underlying activity and are more likely to quit after breaking one; a break is less demotivating if caused by an app malfunction than by one's own forgetting. [Weak, secondary] — [The Decision Lab](https://thedecisionlab.com/fr-CA/insights/consumer-insights/streak-creep-the-perils-of-too-much-gamification)
- Practitioner view (Yu-kai Chou): streaks shift from motivating accomplishment to loss avoidance around day 30; recommends capped streaks (e.g., 7-day cycles) and grace periods; says monetising streak anxiety boosts short-term revenue but erodes trust. [Practitioner] — [Yu-kai Chou](https://yukaichou.com/?p=38864)
- App patterns: Stoic lets users restore a streak after a day or two missed (Premium); Apple Journal's streak/words/days summary is user-enabled; Day One shows streaks on all tiers; Finch's missed-day handling is reported inconsistently (soft/pausable). [Vendor/Weak] — see Q5 sources.
- APA advisory: developers should add nudges encouraging breaks and avoid engagement-maximising designs likened to infinite scroll; AI should not persuade users away from real-life conversations. [Moderate] — [advisory PDF](https://www.infocop.es/wp-content/uploads/2025/11/health-advisory-ai-chatbots-wellness-apps-mental-health.pdf)
- Anecdotal accounts of guilt and abandonment after streak breaks (Duolingo-style) exist but are not controlled data. [Weak/anecdotal] — [Streak Anxiety blog](https://habitdoom.com/blog/streak-anxiety-habit-trackers)

### Inferences
- A "day streak" counter on Lexi's journal screen is likely to help engagement for some users and harm others; for a population where shame is a central emotional theme (Q1), the downside risk is higher. Safer options: show total days journaled (non-consecutive) instead of consecutive streak, make the streak opt-in/hideable, add built-in repair/pause ("rest day", "welcome back") rather than resetting to zero, and never use guilt copy.
- "Words written" is a metric tied to writing volume; for emoji-first or voice users it may signal inadequacy. Consider replacing with "check-ins" or "feelings named," or making it optional (design hypothesis).
- Reminders should be user-set, low-frequency, non-judgemental in tone ("a quiet moment, whenever you're ready"), with easy snooze/off; Guo's finding that closer spacing helps (1-3 days) supports gentle recurring prompts but not nagging.
- Break nudges belong to the AI chat (per APA) more than to the journaling flow.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed study on streak-induced anxiety/guilt in mood-tracking or mental-health apps found; no dyslexia/neurodivergent-specific streak research found.
- Notification-fatigue and optimal-frequency evidence for mental-health apps not retrieved.
- Silverman & Barasch full text not read; per-study effect sizes unknown.
- Finch's and Stoic's streak rules should be confirmed in-app.
