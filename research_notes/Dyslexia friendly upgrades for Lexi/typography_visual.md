# Evidence-based typography, layout and visual presentation for dyslexic readers (applied to the Lexi mobile journaling chatbot)

Conventions used below. Evidence strength flags: **STRONG** (replicated, controlled, adequate N), **MODERATE** (several consistent studies, some limits), **MIXED** (studies disagree), **WEAK** (single small/unreplicated study or expert opinion only), **MYTH** (reviews or replications show no benefit beyond placebo or the claim is unsupported). Population labels: [ADULT], [TEEN] (about 11-18), [CHILD] (under about 11). Items marked "(secondary)" were read only through a search-tool summary or a third-party page, not the primary paper. Items marked "(primary)" were read in the original paper or official page by me. Google Scholar and ACM DL were not directly reachable (403/captcha); PubMed abstracts, PMC XML, author-hosted PDFs and W3C pages were.

## 1. Fonts: OpenDyslexic, Dyslexie, Lexend, Atkinson Hyperlegible, Verdana, Arial, sans vs serif, italics and bold

### Takeaway
No dyslexia-specific font (OpenDyslexic, Dyslexie, Lexend, Atkinson Hyperlegible) has independent, peer-reviewed evidence of a reading-speed or accuracy benefit; where Dyslexie looked helpful, the benefit came from its wider spacing, not its letter shapes (STRONG that letterforms don't matter, MYTH that "dyslexia fonts" help). Verdana/Arial/Helvetica are reasonable, preferred defaults but are not demonstrably faster than other plain fonts; the one font-style finding that holds up is "avoid italics" (MODERATE, via fixation durations and a significantly slower Arial Italic).

### Cited Findings

**Rello & Baeza-Yates 2013 (ASSETS) "Good Fonts for Dyslexia" [mixed TEEN/ADULT] (primary)**
- Design: 48 people with confirmed dyslexia, ages 11-50 (mean 20.96), read 12 comparable ~60-word Spanish texts, one per font (Arial, Arial Italic, CMU, Courier, Garamond, Helvetica, Myriad, OpenDyslexic, OpenDyslexic Italic, Times, Times Italic, Verdana); eye-tracked; all texts left-justified, 14 pt, column width at most 70 characters, black on white; one literal comprehension question as control; subjective 5-point preference ratings — [Rello & Baeza-Yates 2013 PDF](https://www.changedyslexia.org/publications/pdfs/2013-ASSETS-Good%20Fonts%20for%20Dyslexia.pdf)
- The abstract headline ("sans serif, monospaced and roman fonts significantly improved reading performance") rests on **fixation duration**, not reading time. On reading time there was no significant effect of italics (W=4556, p=0.09), serif (p=0.2021) or monospace (p=0.159). The authors themselves write "our conclusions towards these characteristics are weaker" and that only 16 of 66 pairwise reading-time comparisons were significant — [same PDF](https://www.changedyslexia.org/publications/pdfs/2013-ASSETS-Good%20Fonts%20for%20Dyslexia.pdf)
- Mean reading time (index vs Arial = 100): Arial 28.35 s (100, fastest), OpenDyslexic 29.17 (103), CMU 29.58 (104), Courier 29.61 (104), Helvetica 31.05 (109), Verdana 31.16 (110), Times 31.68 (112), Garamond 33.30 (117), Arial Italic 34.99 (123, slowest shown) — so Verdana was numerically ~10% slower than Arial; the authors say Arial "had the shortest reading time" but "we cannot conclude that this font type leads to better readability because we only found significant differences with respect to OpenDys It. and Arial It." — [same PDF](https://www.changedyslexia.org/publications/pdfs/2013-ASSETS-Good%20Fonts%20for%20Dyslexia.pdf)
- Fixation duration means: sans serif 0.24 s vs serif 0.26 s (p=0.008); monospaced 0.22 s vs proportional 0.26 s (p<0.001); italic 0.27 s vs roman 0.25 s (p=0.040). Courier had the lowest fixation duration; Helvetica third lowest; Arial Italic highest. Arial Italic had significantly longer reading time than Arial (p=0.011), CMU (p=0.011) and Helvetica (p=0.034). Authors: "Arial It. should be avoided" — [same PDF](https://www.changedyslexia.org/publications/pdfs/2013-ASSETS-Good%20Fonts%20for%20Dyslexia.pdf)
- Preferences: participants significantly preferred Verdana and Helvetica (Verdana > OpenDyslexic p=0.002; Verdana > Arial Italic p<0.001), and disliked Garamond. Preference vs reading time Pearson r = -0.13, "almost no correlation" — [same PDF](https://www.changedyslexia.org/publications/pdfs/2013-ASSETS-Good%20Fonts%20for%20Dyslexia.pdf)
- OpenDyslexic: "did not lead to a better or worse readability" (103% of Arial's reading time, not significant); participants significantly preferred Verdana or Helvetica over it. The authors note they did not test word-list reading aloud, where specialised fonts might help — [same PDF](https://www.changedyslexia.org/publications/pdfs/2013-ASSETS-Good%20Fonts%20for%20Dyslexia.pdf)
- Authors' "good fonts" (performance + preference): Helvetica, Courier, Arial, Verdana, CMU. Caveats: Spanish text from one novel; 14 pt; desktop eye tracker; a later critique (secondary) argues letter spacing was not controlled across typefaces, so the sans-serif advantage may partly reflect spacing — [Springer chapter](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-030-90984-0_13)
- A 2016 TACCESS follow-up on screen readability (reported as 97 Spanish speakers, 48 dyslexic; secondary, not retrieved) is cited as listing Helvetica, Courier, Arial and CMU as best — [review page](https://a11y-paradise.onrender.com/reviews/69cd7af2bbd7e71582e41407)

**OpenDyslexic**
- Wery & Diliberto 2017 (Annals of Dyslexia) [CHILD]: single-subject alternating-treatment design, 12 students recruited (11 completed), about 9-12 years old (grades 3-6) at a dyslexia school; 1-minute letter-naming, real-word and nonsense-word reading in OpenDyslexic vs Arial vs Times New Roman. No font produced a stronger trend; as extracted from the full text, improvement-rate-difference effect sizes were negative for OpenDyslexic vs both baselines on all three tasks for both fluency and accuracy; "no evidence" it improves speed/accuracy; some students called it "new/different", none preferred it; Arial vs TNR showed no practical difference; limitations: no comprehension measure, small N — [Wery & Diliberto, PMC5629233](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5629233)
- Rello & Baeza-Yates 2013 [TEEN/ADULT]: OpenDyslexic neutral (above). Verdict: **MYTH** that it helps; harmless as an optional preference.

**Dyslexie**
- Marinus et al. 2016 (Dyslexia 22(3):233-244) [CHILD, English]: 39 low-progress readers read four texts matched on x-height; Dyslexie gave 7% more words per minute than standard-spaced Arial, but when Arial's within-word and between-word spacing was matched to Dyslexie's the difference was no longer significant. Conclusion: benefit is "because of its particular spacing settings", not letter shapes — [PubMed 27194598](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27194598/)
- Kuster et al. 2018 (Annals of Dyslexia 68:25-42) [CHILD, Dutch]: Exp. 1, 170 children with dyslexia: Dyslexie not faster or more accurate than Arial; majority preferred Arial; preference unrelated to performance. Exp. 2: 102 with and 45 without dyslexia read word lists in Dyslexie, Arial, Times New Roman: no benefit, preferred Arial/TNR. Conclusion: Dyslexie "neither benefits nor impedes" reading — [PubMed 29204931](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29204931/)
- Galliussi et al. 2020 (Annals of Dyslexia 70:141-152) [TEEN, mean age 12.4 ± 1, Italian]: 64 children with dyslexia + 64 age-matched controls read 8 texts aloud, crossing "dyslexia-friendly" letterform (EasyReading) x inter-letter spacing x inter-word spacing: no effect of letterform; speed was impaired by increased inter-letter spacing that was not accompanied by adequate increase in inter-word spacing — [PMC7188700](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7188700/)
- Machado et al. 2025 (Behavior Analysis in Practice) [ADULT]: evaluated Dyslexie and other fonts in adults with dyslexia; no significant differences in reading accuracy or efficiency across font types; satisfaction/preference varied (abstract only; sample size not retrieved) — [PubMed 41523822](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41523822/)
- Verdict: **MYTH** for letterforms; **MODERATE** that the active ingredient is spacing (see Section 2).

**Lexend**
- I found no independent peer-reviewed study of Lexend in dyslexic readers. Claims trace to the designer's doctoral work (Bonnie Shaver-Troup, second-grade oral reading fluency; journal/peer-review status could not be confirmed), Lexend's own site, and blogs. An Adobe blog's "19.8% fluency gain" is itself attributed to a 2022 non-study classroom source; a Teleprompter blog cites a words-correct-per-minute advantage over Times New Roman (p=0.014) without naming the study — [Lexend site](https://lexend.com/); [Adobe blog](https://www.adobe.com/express/learn/blog/what-is-lexend-font); [Teleprompter blog](https://teleprompter.com/blog/effectiveness-of-lexend-and-opendyslexic-fonts); [Keeler blog on Shaver-Troup](https://alicekeeler.com/2020/07/17/use-lexend-to-improve-reading-by-bshavertroupedd/). All secondary/promotional. Lexend is a wide, generously spaced font, so any benefit is plausibly the spacing effect. Verdict: **WEAK/unproven**.

**Atkinson Hyperlegible**
- Designed by the Braille Institute for low-vision readers (distinct I/l/1, non-mirrored b/d/p/q); I found no peer-reviewed readability study and no dyslexia study. A CSUN 2024 session abstract promised "results from a quantitative study with low-vision readers", but the results were not retrievable. The 2025 "Next" release announcement is marketing copy — [CSUN 2024 session](https://www.csun.edu/cod/conference/sessions/2024/index.php/public/presentations/view/3108.html); [Adobe explainer](https://www.adobe.com/express/learn/blog/atkinson-hyperlegible-font). Verdict: **WEAK** (design rationale only).

**Individual fonts vs. speed in general adults (not dyslexia-specific)**
- Wallace et al. 2022 (ACM TOCHI) [ADULT, crowdsourced, N=352 after exclusions, not a dyslexia sample]: 16 fonts with sizes normalised by perceived size; reading speed rose 35% between each person's fastest and slowest font with no comprehension loss; "no single factor predicts reading speed ... preference does not predict speed"; mean speed in participants' fastest font 314 WPM vs 275 WPM in their most-preferred font; highest-rated font 283 WPM vs 271 average — [Wallace et al. PDF](https://jeffhuang.com/papers/Readability_TOCHI22.pdf)

**Sans vs serif**
- Reading time not significantly different (Rello & Baeza-Yates 2013, above); Arial vs Times New Roman showed no practical difference in Wery & Diliberto; Schneps et al. 2013 used Times (14 pt on paper, 42 pt on the handheld) and found benefits from short lines, so serif is not disqualifying when lines are short and type is large — [Schneps et al. 2013, PLOS ONE](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0075634). BDA still recommends sans serif ("letters can appear less crowded") — [BDA Style Guide 2023 web copy](https://www.bumc.bu.edu/jmedday/files/2025/05/Dyslexia-friendly-style-guide.docx). Verdict: sans serif **WEAK-to-MODERATE** (fixation-duration advantage only; expert consensus).

**Italics and bold**
- Italics: slower reading time (Arial Italic 123% of Arial) and longer fixations (0.27 vs 0.25 s) — Rello & Baeza-Yates 2013 (above). BDA: "avoid underlining and italics as this can make the text appear to run together and cause crowding. Use bold for emphasis." — [BDA Style Guide 2023 web copy](https://www.bumc.bu.edu/jmedday/files/2025/05/Dyslexia-friendly-style-guide.docx). Verdict on italics: **MODERATE**. 
- Bold for emphasis: recommended by BDA only; I found no experiment on bold in dyslexic readers (see Gaps). Verdict: **WEAK** (expert consensus).

### Inferences
- Lexi's default Verdana is defensible (preferred by dyslexic readers in the best-known font study, listed by BDA, mid-pack on speed); there is no evidence that switching the default to Lexend or Atkinson would improve reading. Keep them as user-selectable, but do not market them as clinically proven.
- The "wide spacing" explanation (Marinus, Galliussi, Kuster) implies Lexi's biggest typographic lever is spacing controls, not font choice (see Section 2).
- Italic styling should be avoided in bot and user messages (e.g., for quoted text, system notes, "typing..." states); use bold or a separate bubble style instead.
- Because preference does not predict speed (r = -0.13 in Rello; no relation in Kuster; Wallace), "users pick what looks nice" is a comfort/autonomy argument, not a performance argument.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed Lexend or Atkinson Hyperlegible study with dyslexic participants found; Shaver-Troup dissertation not located.
- No experiment on bold weight, font weight (light/regular/medium) or x-height in dyslexic readers found.
- Rello & Baeza-Yates 2016 TACCESS (screen readability by font) not retrieved; the 97-participant figure and rankings are unverified.
- Almost no font study uses phone-sized screens; most are desktop eye-tracking or paper.
- Evidence base in this section is mostly children and Spanish/Dutch/Italian text; limited English-adult data (Machado 2025 abstract only).

## 2. Spacing: letter, word, line; font size; line length / characters per line

### Takeaway
Extra letter spacing is the best-supported spacing intervention for people with dyslexia, with the strongest evidence in children and teens and smaller/mixed effects in adults; it must be paired with proportionally larger word spacing (MODERATE). Larger type helps (MODERATE; 18 pt/about 24 px is the most-cited value). Line spacing has little measured effect (WEAK, but harmless). Line length is contested: BDA says 60-70 characters, while the best handheld/mobile evidence supports very short lines (about 2-3 words per line) for the subset of readers with visual-attention problems (MIXED).

### Cited Findings

**Letter spacing**
- Zorzi et al. 2012 (PNAS 109:11455) [CHILD/TEEN, ages 8-14, mean 10.4]: Exp. 1, 74 dyslexic children (34 Italian, 40 French) read 24 short unrelated sentences (high-frequency words) aloud on paper, 14 pt Times-Roman; spaced text added **+2.5 pt** to letter spacing (about 0.18 em at 14 pt; e.g., 2.7 pt gap became 5.2 pt) and also proportionally increased word and line spacing. Errors were roughly halved ("improved accuracy by a factor of two"); in the reading-level-matched comparison, speed for dyslexic children rose from 1.75 to 1.94 syllables/s (about +11%) while matched younger controls did not change (1.87 vs 1.93). Exp. 2 (20 Italian dyslexic children, isolated letter-spacing change): errors 6 vs 11.8, speed 1.87 vs 1.64 syllables/s; benefit correlated with poor letter identification (r = -0.60). Press coverage reports about 20% faster reading and halved errors — [Zorzi et al. PNAS 2012 PDF](https://ccnl.psy.unipd.it/publications/publications_folder/extra-large-letter-spacing-improves-reading-in-dyslexia/at_download/file); [PubMed 22665803](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22665803/); [ScienceDaily](https://sciencedaily.com/releases/2012/06/120607105712.htm) (secondary). (A secondary summary gave N=74 total; the primary paper reports 74 in Exp. 1 plus 20 in Exp. 2.)
- Perea et al. 2012 (Learning and Instruction 22:420) [CHILD, Spanish]: +1.2 pt spacing in Times New Roman helped children with dyslexia (faster reading, better comprehension) but not typically developing children — as summarised in [Perea et al. 2016 PDF](https://www.uv.es/mperea/interletter_SJP.pdf) (secondary for the 2012 paper; primary for the 2016 paper).
- Sjoblom, Eaton & Stagg 2016 (Br J Educ Psychol 86:630) [ADULT]: 24 dyslexic and 24 non-dyslexic university students, matched on age and fluid IQ; large letter spacing improved reading speed in general and accuracy in dyslexic readers — [PubMed 27629067](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27629067/)
- Stagg & Kiss 2021 (Res Dev Disabil 119:104065) [TEEN, mean age 13]: 32 dyslexic and 27 non-dyslexic adolescents; extra-large letter spacing improved reading speed more for the dyslexia group and reduced missed-word errors; coloured overlays had no significant effect — [PubMed 34600780](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34600780/)
- Perea et al. 2016 (Spanish J Psychology 19:e26) [ADULT, skilled non-dyslexic readers]: +1.2 pt gave shorter fixations (237 vs 245 ms) but more fixations, so total reading time did not change; comprehension unchanged; Bayes factor 3.67 in favour of the null; only 44% of readers were faster with extra spacing. Authors conclude spacing "does not seem to play a consistently facilitative role" for skilled adults; an unpublished Tai et al. 2009 study (-1.75 to +2.0 pt, 10 pt Verdana) also found equal total reading times — [Perea et al. 2016 PDF](https://www.uv.es/mperea/interletter_SJP.pdf)
- Galliussi et al. 2020 [TEEN]: wider letter spacing without wider word spacing slowed reading (Section 1) — [PMC7188700](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7188700/). The paper also notes Joo et al. 2018 identified a subgroup with elevated crowding who read faster with increased inter-letter and inter-word spacing, i.e., the effect is not uniform across people with dyslexia — [PMC7188700](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7188700/)
- Marinus 2016: spacing explains Dyslexie's small 7% advantage [CHILD] — [PubMed 27194598](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27194598/)
- Rello & Baeza-Yates 2017 (Univ Access Inf Soc 16:29-49): eye tracking, 92 people (46 with dyslexia, 46 controls); "larger text and larger character spacings lead the participants with and without dyslexia to read significantly faster" (abstract as reported; full text not retrieved) — [UPF repository](https://repositori.upf.edu/items/e0ca6f40-a0fa-490f-9d03-53d043270deb) (secondary). In the same group's earlier 22-participant study, column width, line, word and paragraph spacing did not reach significance — [Rello et al. iOS reader paper (citing W4A 2012)](https://www.changedyslexia.org/publications/pdfs/2013-ASSETS-An%20iOS%20Reader%20Dyslexia.pdf)

**Official numeric spacing guidance**
- BDA (Dyslexia Style Guide 2023, web text): inter-letter/character spacing "ideally around 35% of the average letter width. If letter spacing is excessive it can reduce readability"; inter-word spacing "at least 3.5 times the inter-letter spacing"; line spacing "proportional to inter-word spacing; 1.5/150% is preferable"; add extra space around headings and between paragraphs — [BDA 2023 web copy](https://www.bumc.bu.edu/jmedday/files/2025/05/Dyslexia-friendly-style-guide.docx). (The 2023 PDF itself returned 403 for me; this is a copy of the BDA page text, which links to "Dyslexia Style Guide 2023 (PDF)".)
- WCAG 2.2 SC 1.4.12 Text Spacing (AA): content must not lose information/function when the user sets line height at least 1.5x font size, paragraph spacing at least 2x, letter spacing at least 0.12x, word spacing at least 0.16x. These are minimums content must tolerate, not mandated author values. Basis: McLeish 2007 tested 0.04-0.25 em letter spacing; reading speed increased with spacing and levelled off around 0.20 em; Wayne Dick analysed the data; rationale text explicitly cites dyslexia ("Some readers increase spacing to read faster") — [W3C Understanding 1.4.12](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/text-spacing.html)
- Smashing Magazine's practitioner write-up of a dyslexia mode (secondary) uses line-height 2.0 and notes the BDA "35% of average letter width" and "3.5x" figures; it recommends WCAG 4.5:1 contrast and avoiding justification — [Smashing Magazine 2021](https://www.smashingmagazine.com/2021/11/dyslexia-friendly-mode-website/)

**Line spacing**
- Rello et al. 2013 (W4A, "Size matters (spacing not)") [ADULT/TEEN, 28 dyslexic participants, Spanish Wikipedia articles]: font sizes 10, 12, 14, 18, 22, 26 pt and line spacing 0.8, 1.0, 1.4, 1.8 tested; size affected readability, line spacing did not; recommended 18 pt; fixation durations at 18 pt were shorter than at 10 pt (p<0.01) and 26 pt (p<0.05) — (secondary summary) [Unpaywall record](https://unpaywall.org/10.1145%2F2461121.2461125); the same group's iOS reader paper repeats "sizes ranging from 18 points to 26 points lead to faster readings" — [iOS reader paper](https://www.changedyslexia.org/publications/pdfs/2013-ASSETS-An%20iOS%20Reader%20Dyslexia.pdf)

**Font size**
- BDA: 12-14 pt "or equivalent (e.g. 1-1.2em / 16-19 px). Some dyslexic readers may request a larger font" — [BDA 2023 web copy](https://www.bumc.bu.edu/jmedday/files/2025/05/Dyslexia-friendly-style-guide.docx)
- Rello & Bigham 2017 used 18-point Arial for their colour study, citing the 18 pt finding — [Rello & Bigham PDF](https://www.changedyslexia.org/publications/pdfs/2017-ASSETS-Good%20Background%20Colors.pdf)
- Schneps et al. used 42 pt Times New Roman on an iPod Touch held about 35 cm away (8-character word subtending about 3.6 degrees) — [Schneps et al. 2013](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0075634)

**Line length / characters per line**
- BDA (both the older ~2012-2018 version and the 2023 web copy): "60 to 70 characters is optimal"; avoid multiple/narrow newspaper-style columns; left-align without justification — [BDA 2023 web copy](https://www.bumc.bu.edu/jmedday/files/2025/05/Dyslexia-friendly-style-guide.docx); [older BDA guide as hosted by Wigan Council](https://www.wigan.gov.uk/Docs/PDF/Business/Professionals/SEN/HEFA/Dyslexia-Style-Guide.pdf)
- Rello & Baeza-Yates 2013 capped columns at 70 characters "as recommended by the British Association of Dyslexia" (they did not test line length) — [PDF](https://www.changedyslexia.org/publications/pdfs/2013-ASSETS-Good%20Fonts%20for%20Dyslexia.pdf). A W4A 2012 design varied column width across 22/44/66/88 characters with no reported significant effect (secondary: [Rello iOS paper](https://www.changedyslexia.org/publications/pdfs/2013-ASSETS-An%20iOS%20Reader%20Dyslexia.pdf); widths from search result).
- Schneps et al. 2013 (PLOS ONE 8:e75634) [TEEN, 103 high-school students with dyslexia in Boston]: reading on a small handheld e-reader (iPod Touch) formatted to about 3.40 (SD 0.91) words per line (42 pt, left-justified ragged right, gray text at 75% white on black background, scrolled vertically) vs paper at 13.94 words per line (14 pt Times). Significant gains only in subgroups: those with the worst phoneme-decoding/sight-word efficiency read faster on the device, and those with limited visual-attention span gained in comprehension. Authors attribute the effect to short lines, not the device; lead author says about a third of the students tested showed these visual-attention issues and "for those who don't have these issues ... traditional ways of displaying text are better". In their earlier eye-tracking study, 2.19 vs 11.6 words per line halved regressive saccades and raised reading speed 27% — [Schneps et al. 2013](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0075634); [press summary of the caveat](https://www.healthday.com/health-news/child-health/e-readers-benefit-some-with-dyslexia-study-finds-680220.html) (secondary)
- Practitioner note (older BDA guide): "readers access text at a 25% slower rate on a computer" for web — [Wigan copy of BDA guide](https://www.wigan.gov.uk/Docs/PDF/Business/Professionals/SEN/HEFA/Dyslexia-Style-Guide.pdf)

### Inferences
- Zorzi's +2.5 pt at 14 pt is about +0.18 em; BDA's "35% of average letter width" is roughly +0.17 em if the average letter is about 0.5 em wide; WCAG's 0.12 em floor and McLeish's ~0.20 em plateau bracket the same region. So a letter-spacing setting of about 0 / +0.05 / +0.12 / +0.18 em looks like a research-consistent range for Lexi, always with a proportional word-spacing increase (Galliussi: letter spacing alone can hurt).
- Evidence for letter spacing is mostly children/teens reading aloud in short controlled passages; adults with dyslexia show benefit in at least one study (Sjoblom), skilled non-dyslexic adults show none in total reading time (Perea). So offer it as an option and a modest default at most, not a one-size mandate.
- Lexi's 16-23 px range tops out at about 17 pt (23 px x 0.75). Rello's 18 pt is 24 px; adding a 24 px (or larger) step is consistent with the evidence (note px vs pt differ by viewing distance on phones; none of the studies used phone-held viewing distances except Schneps' iPod).
- Line spacing options of 1.5, 1.7, 2.0 already meet or exceed BDA/WCAG; research suggests more line spacing yields little extra, so it is not a priority.
- Lexi's bubbles at about 64% of a ~375 px-wide phone are about 240 px wide, i.e., roughly 18-26 characters (about 3-5 words) per line at 16-23 px in Verdana (assuming about 0.55-0.6 em average character width). That falls in the "short lines" regime of Schneps (2-3.4 words/line), far below BDA's 60-70 character ideal. The two guidelines conflict; the Schneps benefit was subgroup-specific. Making bubble width adjustable (and testing narrow vs wide) is better than assuming either.

### Gaps
- Exact BDA 2023 PDF wording not accessed (403); relied on a copy of the BDA web page. The older guide (2012-2018) says "60 to 70 characters" too, so that number appears stable.
- No study isolates the number of characters per line on a phone for adults with dyslexia; Schneps is the only handheld evidence and used teens, large type and scrolling text.
- Rello's W4A 2012/2013 papers and the 2017 UAIS full text were not retrieved; details come from abstracts or later citations.
- No evidence on word-spacing-only manipulations, or on "optimal" paragraph spacing, in dyslexia beyond WCAG's engineering minimum.

## 3. Colour and contrast: background colours, off-white vs white, glare, dark mode, coloured overlays / Irlen / Meares-Irlen

### Takeaway
One large study (Rello & Bigham 2017) found warm backgrounds (peach, orange, yellow) with black text produced faster reading than cool ones (blue-grey, blue, green) in people with and without dyslexia, but it did not test plain white and has not been replicated (WEAK-to-MODERATE). Coloured overlays/lenses and Irlen "treatment" are not supported by systematic reviews: benefits are small, placebo-sized and at high risk of bias (MYTH as a dyslexia intervention). Dark mode has no dyslexia-specific trials; general evidence favours dark-on-light for reading performance but preferences vary (WEAK/MIXED).

### Cited Findings

**Rello & Bigham 2017 (ASSETS '17) "Good Background Colors for Readers" [ADULT] (primary)**
- 341 participants (89 with dyslexia or at risk: 66 with confirmed diagnosis, 23 suspected; 252 controls), all adults aged 18-60, Spanish native speakers, mostly highly educated (83% college degree or higher). Ten comparable 55-word paragraphs, each on one of 10 background colours with black text, 18-pt Arial, left-justified; supervised online sessions; measures: reading time, mouse distance, literal comprehension as control — [Rello & Bigham PDF](https://www.changedyslexia.org/publications/pdfs/2017-ASSETS-Good%20Background%20Colors.pdf)
- Tested colours (hex; WCAG contrast with black, as reported by the authors and which I re-computed and matched exactly): Blue #96ADFC 9.68:1; Blue Grey #DBE1F1 16.05:1; Green #A8F29A 15.83:1; Grey #D8D3D6 14.21:1; Orange #EDDD6E 15.17:1; Peach #EDD1B0 14.35:1; Purple #B987DC 7.56:1; Red #E0A6AA 10.2:1; Turquoise #A5F7E1 16.99:1; Yellow #F8FD89 19.4:1. All exceed WCAG AAA (7:1). **Pure white was not one of the ten conditions.** — [same PDF](https://www.changedyslexia.org/publications/pdfs/2017-ASSETS-Good%20Background%20Colors.pdf)
- Results: Peach, Orange, Yellow gave the shortest reading times and shortest mouse travel; Blue Grey, Blue, Green the longest. Dyslexia group mean reading time: Peach 14.85 s (index 100), Orange 15.33 s (103), Yellow 16.30 s (109), Blue Grey 21.57 s (145, slowest), Blue second slowest. Control group: Peach 12.28 s (100), Orange 12.32 (100), Yellow 13.43 (109), Turquoise 14.54 (118), Purple 14.68 (119), Red 14.97 (121), Grey 16.03 (130), Blue 16.24 (132), Green 16.45 (133), Blue Grey 18.82 (153). Group colour-rankings correlate rho = 0.964 (reading time) and 0.794 (mouse distance). Dyslexic readers were slower overall (17.73 vs 14.98 s, p<0.001). Authors: the impact "is comparable for both groups" — [same PDF](https://www.changedyslexia.org/publications/pdfs/2017-ASSETS-Good%20Background%20Colors.pdf)
- Authors' own caveats: results "do not necessarily mean that warm colors are recommended"; some readers may prefer cool colours that relax them; preference was not measured; cool colours "do not necessarily need to be avoided" — [same PDF](https://www.changedyslexia.org/publications/pdfs/2017-ASSETS-Good%20Background%20Colors.pdf)
- Consistent earlier work by the same group: eye tracking with 22 dyslexic readers (and later 92 people, 46 dyslexic) found black on cream gave the shortest fixation durations; BDA recommends cream — [same PDF](https://www.changedyslexia.org/publications/pdfs/2017-ASSETS-Good%20Background%20Colors.pdf)
- Because contrast ratios of winners (14.35-19.4:1) and losers (Blue Grey 16.05:1, Green 15.83:1) overlap, hue/saturation rather than contrast ratio differentiates the conditions (computed contrast values: [same PDF](https://www.changedyslexia.org/publications/pdfs/2017-ASSETS-Good%20Background%20Colors.pdf)).

**Off-white vs white, glare**
- BDA: "Use dark coloured text on a light (not white) background", "Consider alternatives to white backgrounds ... White can appear too dazzling. Use cream or a soft pastel colour. Some dyslexic people will have their own colour preference"; single-colour backgrounds, avoid patterns; "sufficient contrast"; avoid green and red/pink for colour-vision deficiencies — [BDA 2023 web copy](https://www.bumc.bu.edu/jmedday/files/2025/05/Dyslexia-friendly-style-guide.docx)
- Rello & Bigham cite practitioner claims (Perron; Bradford) that very high contrast "creates so much vibration that it diminishes readability" and recommend off-black/off-white pairs — these are opinion citations, not experiments — [Rello & Bigham PDF](https://www.changedyslexia.org/publications/pdfs/2017-ASSETS-Good%20Background%20Colors.pdf). I found no controlled study isolating white vs cream for dyslexic readers.
- A CAST brief (secondary) says contrast ratio affects visual performance more than colour in typical-vision college students and hypothesises the same for dyslexia — [CAST brief](https://www.cast.org/binaries/content/assets/cast/downloads/cisl-features-color-contrast-202308-a11y.pdf)
- Schneps et al.'s handheld condition used grey text (75% of full white) on a black background to enlarge effective margins — a dark, reduced-contrast presentation was part of the one condition that helped a subgroup, but it was confounded with short lines — [Schneps et al. 2013](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0075634)
- COGA: use solid backgrounds for text blocks and "strong color contrast"; avoid text over busy backgrounds — [W3C COGA Making Content Usable, pattern 4.4.11](https://www.w3.org/TR/coga-usable/)
- WCAG minimum contrast 4.5:1 (cited by Smashing) — [Smashing Magazine 2021](https://www.smashingmagazine.com/2021/11/dyslexia-friendly-mode-website/). Lexi's black on #E6E6E6 = 16.83:1 (my calculation, WCAG relative-luminance formula; same formula reproduced all ten of Rello & Bigham's published ratios).

**Dark mode / negative polarity**
- I found no dyslexia-specific controlled study of dark mode. General literature, per a literature review at Oslo Metropolitan University (secondary), mostly recommends positive polarity (dark text on light) for reading tasks, with negative polarity helping only in some visual-impairment cases — [OsloMet review PDF](https://oda.oslomet.no/oda-xmlui/bitstream/handle/10642/9803/LightDark%20RV16.pdf?sequence=1). Nielsen Norman Group (secondary summary) reports light mode better for proofreading and acuity tasks across ages — [NN/g dark mode](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/dark-mode/).
- Counter-evidence (small, non-dyslexic): 30 young adults read faster with negative polarity (136.27 wpm vs 128.42 wpm) — [JHSMR 2024 (secondary)](https://www.jhsmr.org/index.php/jhsmr/article/view/1095). A small adolescent eye-tracking pre-study (some adolescents with language-based learning needs) suggested light text on dark may improve speed — [Training, Language and Culture 2017 (secondary)](https://doaj.org/article/cbe1e03f766f41d1b1d7a799f3986dc2).
- Verdict: **WEAK/MIXED**; treat as a preference, not an upgrade.

**Coloured overlays / Irlen / Meares-Irlen syndrome**
- Griffiths, Taylor, Henderson & Barrett 2016 (Ophthalmic Physiol Opt 36:519-544): systematic review of 51 published items (54 data sets) across Intuitive, Irlen, Harris/Chromagen and other systems, rated with the Cochrane Risk of Bias tool: most studies at "high" or "uncertain" risk of bias; studies at lower risk "provide less support"; effect sizes "generally small and/or similar to the improvement found with a placebo"; conclusion: use of coloured lenses or overlays to ameliorate reading difficulties "cannot be endorsed" and reported individual benefits are "likely to be the result of placebo, practice or Hawthorne effects" — [PubMed 27580753](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27580753/)
- Stagg & Kiss 2021 [TEEN]: coloured overlays had no significant effect on reading speed or errors, whereas letter spacing did — [PubMed 34600780](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34600780/)
- Miyasaka et al. 2019 (Arquivos de Neuro-Psiquiatria) systematic review on Irlen syndrome reached a similar view (no evidence of treatment effectiveness) — (secondary) [DOAJ record](https://doaj.org/article/7cc96771702b4bcf99c005ce66b318b5)
- The 2009 joint statement from the American Academy of Pediatrics/American Academy of Ophthalmology/AAPOS says no scientific evidence supports tinted lenses or vision therapy for dyslexia (secondary summaries; full text not retrieved) — [Reading Rockets summary](https://www.readingrockets.org/topics/dyslexia/articles/learning-disabilities-dyslexia-and-vision)
- Rello & Bigham note Meares-Irlen/visual stress is "not a language-based disorder" but reported as comorbid with dyslexia (25.84% in Spanish speakers to 46% in Portuguese speakers per sources they cite); Griffiths et al. note the term is "questioned by many as to its validity as a distinct syndrome" (secondary, via an Optician magazine report of the review) — [Rello & Bigham PDF](https://www.changedyslexia.org/publications/pdfs/2017-ASSETS-Good%20Background%20Colors.pdf); [Optician Online](https://www.opticianonline.net/content/features/in-focus-do-coloured-filters-rely-on-placebo-effect)

### Inferences
- **Verdict on overlays/Irlen: MYTH** as a treatment claim. Griffiths covers clinical endorsement and individually prescribed filters; it does not say that letting users pick a background tint in an app is harmful. Offering a colour choice as personal comfort is low-risk; calling it therapy is not supported.
- **Warm background default (peach/cream): WEAK-to-MODERATE, cheap to try.** The only large study shows the best (peach) vs worst (blue-grey) background differing by 45% in reading time for the dyslexia group and 53% for controls (best warm vs mid-pack colours differed by roughly 9-30%), but: single study, Spanish, adults, online, 55-word paragraphs, no white baseline, dyslexic group partly "at risk". Lexi's current light theme is a neutral grey (#E6E6E6, close to the study's mid-to-slow "Grey" #D8D3D6, which was 130% of Peach in the control group), and its mint/green bubbles resemble the "Green" (#A8F29A) and "Turquoise" (#A5F7E1) conditions which were in the slower half. Bubble backgrounds are small areas, though, so the study doesn't transfer directly. A cream/peach theme would be consistent with BDA and Rello & Bigham.
- Contrast: keep text contrast high but not necessarily maximal (14-19:1 was used by all studied winners); avoid cool blue/blue-grey as a full-screen default; always keep at least WCAG 4.5:1 for dark-theme orange text and bubble text.
- Dark themes: keep as optional modes for light sensitivity/preference; no basis to claim dyslexia benefits.

### Gaps
- No direct white vs cream/off-white experiment for dyslexic readers found; "white is too dazzling" rests on BDA opinion and anecdote.
- No phone-based colour study. Rello & Bigham was desktop browser.
- Dark mode and dyslexia: only small, non-dyslexic or adolescent pre-study data; none retrieved in full.
- A 2026 Chais conference paper (Open University of Israel) reportedly compared cream vs white in Hebrew text with preference for cream among people with reading difficulties (only one dimension significant) — surfaced only in a search summary, not verified: [Chais 2026 PDF](https://www.openu.ac.il/Lists/MediaServer_Documents/innovation/chais/2026/b4_7.pdf).
- AAP/AAO statement text itself not retrieved (secondary only).

## 4. Official guidance: BDA Dyslexia Style Guide (2023), W3C COGA, WCAG 2.2 SC 1.4.12

### Takeaway
All three bodies converge on: plain sans serif, 12-14 pt (16-19 px) or larger, generous spacing, left-aligned ragged-right, no italics/underline/all caps, non-white soft background, short chunks with white space, simple icons/pictograms as support, and user control over presentation. They are expert-consensus documents; Rello & Bigham (2017) point out that BDA-type recommendations "are not based on objective measures collected with large user studies," so treat each item as consensus-strength, not experiment-strength.

### Cited Findings
- **BDA Dyslexia Style Guide 2023** (web version, as hosted by Boston University; links to "Dyslexia Style Guide 2023 (PDF)"): sans serif (Arial, Comic Sans; alternatives Verdana, Tahoma, Century Gothic, Trebuchet, Calibri, Open Sans); 12-14 pt or 1-1.2 em / 16-19 px, larger on request; letter spacing about 35% of average letter width; word spacing at least 3.5x letter spacing; line spacing 1.5 preferred; avoid underline and italics, use bold; avoid all capitals for continuous text; headings at least 20% larger than body; extra space around headings/paragraphs; single-colour background, no patterns; dark text on light (not white) background; cream or soft pastel; some people have their own colour preference; avoid green and red/pink; left-align without justification ("easier to find the start and finish of each line and ensures even spacing between words"); avoid multiple columns; "60 to 70 characters is optimal"; white space to remove clutter; short, active sentences; "Use images to support text ... Pictograms and graphics can help to locate and support information in the text"; bullets and numbering rather than continuous prose; consider alongside WCAG — [BDA 2023 web copy](https://www.bumc.bu.edu/jmedday/files/2025/05/Dyslexia-friendly-style-guide.docx)
- The earlier BDA text additionally says "Boxes and borders can be used for effective emphasis," "Avoid starting a sentence at the end of a line," "Pictograms and graphics help to locate information" — [Wigan copy of older BDA guide](https://www.wigan.gov.uk/Docs/PDF/Business/Professionals/SEN/HEFA/Dyslexia-Style-Guide.pdf)
- **WCAG 2.2 SC 1.4.12 Text Spacing (AA)**: line height at least 1.5x, paragraph spacing at least 2x, letter spacing at least 0.12x, word spacing at least 0.16x of font size must be possible without loss of content; basis McLeish 2007; applies to users overriding spacing (e.g., via bookmarklets/extensions); explicitly mentions dyslexia as a motive — [W3C Understanding 1.4.12](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/text-spacing.html)
- **W3C COGA "Making Content Usable for People with Cognitive and Learning Disabilities"** (Working Group Note): user needs and patterns include 4.2.7 "Use Icons that Help the User" (familiar icons next to important content; "Each icon or symbol should convey a single meaning and be next to the content it relates to"; one icon goes left of text in LTR languages; for multiple symbols in a paragraph "place the symbols above the text"; use personalisation semantics so users can load familiar symbols); 4.3.5 "Break Media into Chunks"; 4.4.10 "Use White Spacing" ("white space has been shown to ease reading difficulties and improve understanding"; let users adjust amount of white space; "white space" means the background colour, not necessarily white); 4.4.11 solid backgrounds, strong contrast, no overlaid busy backgrounds; 4.4.6 clear formatting and punctuation; 4.9.4 "Support a Personalized and Familiar Interface"; user needs: "I need symbols placed above the text to link the meaning of the words with the images", "I need small or short chunks of content ... and a good use of white space", "I need personalized symbols, icons, or pictures that I can recognize immediately, as learning new ones takes a long time", "I need to be able to add more white space between lines, sentences, phrases, and chunks" — [W3C COGA Note](https://www.w3.org/TR/coga-usable/)
- COGA persona/section 2.2 notes people with language-related disabilities "may rely on supporting graphics, icons, and familiar symbols to understand content" — [W3C COGA Note](https://www.w3.org/TR/coga-usable/)
- Dyslexia Scotland (secondary, search snippet) says there is no single dyslexia-friendly font and is less prescriptive than BDA — [Dyslexia Scotland formats guide](https://dyslexiascotland.org.uk/dyslexia-friendly-typed-formats)

### Inferences
- Lexi's current choices already match the main consensus items (sans serif Verdana; sizes 16-23 px covering BDA's 16-19 px; line spacing at least 1.5; left aligned; no justification; emoji as supporting symbols). Gaps versus guidance: no warm/cream background (BDA's "not white"; Lexi's grey is non-white so partially met), no letter-spacing/word-spacing control, maximum size below 18 pt, bubble width far under BDA's 60-70 character figure.
- WCAG 1.4.12 should be treated as a robustness test for Lexi: user-applied spacing overrides (0.12 em letter, 0.16 em word, 1.5 line, 2x paragraph) must not clip text in fixed-width bubbles. In React Native this is a layout-testing concern (bubbles must grow, not truncate).
- COGA's emoji-adjacent guidance (single-meaning icons, next to or above the text, user-personalisable) is the best-sourced design rule for Lexi's emoji-first approach.

### Gaps
- BDA 2023 PDF itself returned 403 and the live BDA style-guide web page returned 404; wording drawn from a copy of the BDA web page text (hosted by Boston University) plus an older copy (Wigan Council). The BDA New Technologies Committee typeface page was not checked.
- BDA does not publish the evidence base behind each number; the "35%" and "3.5x" figures have no cited study in what I read.
- COGA excerpts in this note are limited to patterns I could read; COGA's specific line-length/font numbers (if any) were not found in the text I extracted.

## 5. Bionic Reading, other "fixation" formats, syllable highlighting, colour-coded text

### Takeaway
Bionic Reading (bolding the first letters of words) has no demonstrated benefit and the best controlled eye-tracking studies find no change in speed or fixations (MYTH/WEAK for speed claims); there is no good evidence for dyslexic readers specifically. Syllable colouring and other colour segmentation show inconsistent, mostly accuracy-only or beginner-reader effects in small studies (WEAK/MIXED).

### Cited Findings
- Beelders 2025 (Journal of Eye Movement Research) [ADULT, 53 participants with normal or corrected vision, not a dyslexic sample]: "Bionic font does not significantly change eye movements when reading. Fixation durations, number of fixations and reading speeds were not significantly different"; fixations were spread across the whole word, not just bold leading characters; no help for low-frequency words — [PMC12565662](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12565662/)
- A registered eye-tracking study of 90 skilled adults (reported by ScienceBlog; bolding shortened individual fixations but increased their number, per-word reading time unchanged, overall speed lower) and Snell 2024 in Acta Psychologica, "No, Bionic Reading does not work" (32 participants, 100 paragraphs, no reliable advantage for fast or slow readers) — both seen only via a secondary summary — [ScienceBlog](https://scienceblog.com/s-bionic-reading-bolds-the-first-half-of-each-word-on-the-promise-of-more-efficient-reading-and-a-registered-eye-tracking-study-of-90-skilled-adult-readers-found-the-bolding-moved-their-first/)
- Readwise online test (company blog, not peer reviewed): 2,074 testers read about 2.6 words per minute slower with Bionic formatting, comprehension essentially identical — [Readwise](https://blog.readwise.io/bionic-reading-results/)
- Claims of speed gains come from the vendor and unsourced posts (e.g., a 12-person founder study with a vaguely defined "positive effect"), per a critical summary — [Book Riot](https://bookriot.com/?p=534723) (secondary)
- Colour segmentation: Pinna & Deiana 2018 (i-Perception 9:3) [children and adults, dyslexic and typical]: colouring text by monochrome, word, half-word, syllable or letter changed reading time, errors and comprehension errors in all four groups; abstract reports effects in similar trends across groups (direction for each condition not stated in the abstract) — [PubMed 35145618](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35145618/)
- A French master's thesis on 65 dyslexic children (secondary, via search summary) reported that colourised text (syllabic or vowel) neither improved nor reduced reading speed but improved accuracy, and children preferred coloured texts — [thesis](https://dumas.ccsd.cnrs.fr/dumas-03282114/document) (secondary). Brügelmann 2025 ("Red and blue is my reading book... Is it really helpful to mark syllables by color in beginner reading books?") is a critical review whose summary (secondary, PDF unreadable) says syllable colouring was confirmed for first graders but not older children, with some children disturbed by it — [Brügelmann 2025](https://www.pedocs.de/volltexte/2025/34472/pdf/Bruegelmann_2025_Red_and_blue.pdf)
- Leiden University teaching report on colour-coded sound mapping for dyslexic language students: anecdotal, no sample size or controls; errors returned on plain paper — [Leiden news 2025](https://www.universiteitleiden.nl/en/news/2025/02/colours-and-symbols-to-support-dyslexic-students) (anecdotal only)

### Inferences
- Do not add Bionic-style bolding to Lexi's chat text; it would also conflict with BDA's "use bold for emphasis" because it dilutes emphasis. If ever offered, label it as an experimental preference with no claimed benefit.
- Syllable/colour-coding is a literacy-instruction tool for beginners, not a proven reading-aid for adult/teen journaling; mixing many colours also conflicts with BDA/COGA "single colour background / avoid distraction."
- If Lexi wants a "reading aid", the better-supported levers are spacing and size.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed Bionic Reading study in dyslexic readers found.
- Pinna & Deiana's condition-by-condition directions, and the French thesis and Brügelmann details, were not verified at the primary text.
- No study on syllable highlighting on phone-sized screens for teens/adults.

## 6. Text chunking, alignment (no justification), all caps, underlines, and icon/emoji/pictogram use alongside text

### Takeaway
Left-aligned ragged-right text, no all caps, no underline/italic and short chunks with white space are expert-consensus recommendations with little direct experimental support (WEAK, but low cost and consistent across BDA/COGA). Evidence that pictograms/emoji improve comprehension for dyslexic readers is thin: one small preliminary study of dyslexic teens (N=32) hints at benefit for short messages; driving-sign studies are mixed (WEAK/MIXED).

### Cited Findings
- Alignment: BDA: "Left align text, without justification. This makes it easier to find the start and finish of each line and ensures even spacing between words" — [BDA 2023 web copy](https://www.bumc.bu.edu/jmedday/files/2025/05/Dyslexia-friendly-style-guide.docx). Rello's experiments used left-justified (i.e., left-aligned) text throughout, so none tested justification (primary: [Rello & Baeza-Yates 2013](https://www.changedyslexia.org/publications/pdfs/2013-ASSETS-Good%20Fonts%20for%20Dyslexia.pdf); [Rello & Bigham 2017](https://www.changedyslexia.org/publications/pdfs/2017-ASSETS-Good%20Background%20Colors.pdf)). Smashing notes justification alters letter/word spacing and disables it in its dyslexia mode (practitioner) — [Smashing Magazine](https://www.smashingmagazine.com/2021/11/dyslexia-friendly-mode-website/)
- All caps: BDA says avoid; older BDA guide: "AVOID TEXT IN BLOCK CAPITALS: this is much harder to read" — [BDA 2023 web copy](https://www.bumc.bu.edu/jmedday/files/2025/05/Dyslexia-friendly-style-guide.docx); [Wigan copy](https://www.wigan.gov.uk/Docs/PDF/Business/Professionals/SEN/HEFA/Dyslexia-Style-Guide.pdf). No dyslexia experiment on all caps retrieved.
- Underlines/italics: BDA "make the text appear to run together and cause crowding"; italics evidence in Section 1.
- Chunking / white space: COGA 4.3.5, 4.4.10 and user needs for "small or short chunks" and adjustable white space — [W3C COGA Note](https://www.w3.org/TR/coga-usable/). Short lines (Schneps 2013; 2.19 vs 11.6 words per line, 27% faster in earlier eye-tracking work; subgroup effects) are the closest experimental analogue to chunking — [Schneps et al. 2013](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0075634). BDA: "Avoid ... long, dense paragraphs", prefer bullets/numbering, extra space between paragraphs — [BDA 2023 web copy](https://www.bumc.bu.edu/jmedday/files/2025/05/Dyslexia-friendly-style-guide.docx).
- Emoji / emoticons: Lesniak & Grzybowski 2021 (Frontiers in Psychology 12:693287) [TEEN, preliminary]: 32 pupils aged 11-15 (16 with school-approved dyslexia, 16 controls) read short social-media-style messages (verbal-informative, verbal-emotive, emoticon-informative, emoticon-emotive) and answered comprehension questions. Dyslexic participants were slower on all message types (about 6.0 s vs about 3.95-4.32 s for controls) and less accurate on emoticon-informative messages; both groups answered fastest on emoticon-emotive messages (dyslexic mean 5.72 s) and slowest on verbal-informative (6.07 s). Authors: "tentatively" beneficial role of emoticons, especially non-standard (non-emotional) ones in reading short messages with understanding — exploratory, tiny N — [PMC8374149](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8374149/)
- Emoji/general readers: a Ruhr-Universität Bochum study found sentences with emoji took slightly longer to comprehend, with comprehension slightly (not significantly) better — [RUB press release](https://news.rub.de/english/press-releases/2021-11-11-linguistics-how-we-understand-emoji) (secondary)
- Pictograms: BDA ("Use images to support text ... Pictograms and graphics can help to locate and support information") and COGA ("Use Icons that Help the User", symbols above text) recommend them as support — [BDA 2023 web copy](https://www.bumc.bu.edu/jmedday/files/2025/05/Dyslexia-friendly-style-guide.docx); [W3C COGA Note](https://www.w3.org/TR/coga-usable/). Driving-simulation research (secondary): a Macquarie study with 62 students found drivers with higher dyslexia scores had more trouble understanding signs with words and no images — [APS summary](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/news/motr/the-challenges-of-driving-while-dyslexic.html); a 2018 study on variable message signs found single-word messages gave farther legibility distances and fewer glances than pictograms — [Human Factors 2018](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0018720817751623); a study with 22 adults with dyslexia compared text vs pictogram warning messages (results not retrieved) — [search result](https://fitforthem.unipa.it/subject:Pictogram). Evidence is mixed and not about chat.
- Linköping University student report cautioned that added pictures could compete with decoding (excerpt only; results not shown) — [Linköping report](https://www.ida.liu.se/~arnjo82/diginclude/Rapporter/Emojirapport.pdf) (secondary)

### Inferences
- Lexi's left-aligned bubbles, short messages and emoji-as-support are consistent with BDA/COGA, but the pictogram evidence is "consensus plus tiny-N hint", not proof. Emoji should accompany, not replace, words; the existing "words only" mode is well justified as an opt-out because evidence on benefit is weak and icons that must be learned add load (COGA: "learning new ones takes a long time").
- Follow COGA's placement rules: one emoji per meaning, next to (left of) the sentence it relates to, or above the text when several; avoid emoji clusters inline that break words into pieces (inference from COGA's single-meaning rule and BDA's crowding concern).
- Avoid all caps and underlined link styling inside chat bubbles; use bold or a bubble border for emphasis, which BDA explicitly allows.

### Gaps
- No controlled study found on justification, all-caps or underlines in dyslexic adults/teens; recommendations are consensus-level only.
- No study of emoji-first or emoji-plus-text interfaces for dyslexic adults; the only dyslexia emoticon study is N=32, teens, laboratory social-media-style messages.
- The 22-adult pictogram vs text driving study and the RLOG-listed pictogram studies were not retrieved in full.

## 7. Customisation: is personal choice better than one-size-fits-all?

### Takeaway
Presentation needs differ substantially between individuals, so offering adjustable settings is well supported as an accessibility principle (COGA, BDA, WCAG), and the experimental evidence shows real between-person variability (MODERATE). But what people say they prefer is a poor predictor of what they read fastest, so personalisation should be coupled with sensible evidence-based defaults and, ideally, a quick try-it comparison rather than relying on choice alone (MODERATE).

### Cited Findings
- Wallace et al. 2022 [ADULT, N=352, general population]: fastest vs slowest font differs by 35% within the same person; "preference does not predict speed even when font size is controlled"; "different fonts work best for different people"; mean speed 314 WPM in best font vs 275 WPM in most-preferred font — [Wallace et al. PDF](https://jeffhuang.com/papers/Readability_TOCHI22.pdf)
- Rello & Baeza-Yates 2013 [TEEN/ADULT dyslexic]: preference vs reading time r = -0.13 — [PDF](https://www.changedyslexia.org/publications/pdfs/2013-ASSETS-Good%20Fonts%20for%20Dyslexia.pdf); Kuster et al. 2018 [CHILD]: preference unrelated to performance in both experiments — [PubMed 29204931](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29204931/)
- Heterogeneity of benefit: Zorzi's benefit scales with letter-identification deficits (r = -0.60); Galliussi cites a crowding subgroup; Schneps found short-line benefits only in subgroups (about a third with visual-attention problems) and traditional formatting better for others — [Zorzi 2012 PDF](https://ccnl.psy.unipd.it/publications/publications_folder/extra-large-letter-spacing-improves-reading-in-dyslexia/at_download/file); [PMC7188700](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7188700/); [Schneps 2013](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0075634)
- User-adjusted presentation in a dyslexia-specific app: Rello et al. built DysWebxia, a customisable iOS reader (font size, brightness, colour, character/word/line/paragraph spacing, width) based on their eye-tracking findings; the paper presents design, not an outcome evaluation — [iOS reader paper](https://www.changedyslexia.org/publications/pdfs/2013-ASSETS-An%20iOS%20Reader%20Dyslexia.pdf)
- A study of children with and without dyslexia who selected their own display settings (secondary summary of a Spanish-journal paper, reported N=70; 30 dyslexic, 30 typical): both groups chose similar configurations, favouring high contrast and low text density, with larger font size in the dyslexia group; personalisation gave a small fluency boost without differential benefit for dyslexia and no comprehension gain — [RLOG record](https://revistas.ucm.es/index.php/RLOG/en/article/view/101374) (secondary, abstract not retrieved; counts in the summary are inconsistent with the stated N, treat as unverified)
- Official support for customisation: COGA 4.9.4 "Support a Personalized and Familiar Interface" and 4.4.10 ("Make sure users can also adjust the amount of white space"); BDA: "Some dyslexic people will have their own colour preference"; WCAG 1.4.12 exists so users can override spacing — [W3C COGA Note](https://www.w3.org/TR/coga-usable/); [BDA 2023 web copy](https://www.bumc.bu.edu/jmedday/files/2025/05/Dyslexia-friendly-style-guide.docx); [W3C Understanding 1.4.12](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/text-spacing.html)
- Adults with dyslexia (Rello & Bigham): "people with dyslexia may especially benefit" from good colour choices yet group-level colour ranking was nearly identical with and without dyslexia (rho=0.964), i.e., defaults chosen well help everyone — [Rello & Bigham PDF](https://www.changedyslexia.org/publications/pdfs/2017-ASSETS-Good%20Background%20Colors.pdf)

### Inferences
- Lexi's existing three font options, three line-spacing options, text-size slider and three themes are the right architecture. Highest-value additions per evidence: letter-spacing (with matched word spacing), a larger top text size, a warm/cream theme and an adjustable bubble width.
- Because stated preference isn't a good proxy for speed, consider a 20-30 second "pick what reads easiest" comparison on a sample paragraph (A/B on spacing and size) plus allowing later changes, rather than burying settings; it also doubles as onboarding. Whether this improves outcomes is untested (inference).
- Defaults should be evidence-leaning and conservative (sans serif, 18-20 px, 1.5-1.7 line height, modest letter spacing, non-white warm background), since most users never change defaults (general UX assumption, unsourced).

### Gaps
- No RCT comparing self-selected vs prescribed presentation in dyslexic adults/teens on phones.
- RLOG study details unverified at primary source.
- Wallace et al. is not a dyslexia sample; transferability unknown.

## 8. Synthesis for Lexi: which visual upgrades are supported and which are myths

### Takeaway
Best-supported upgrades: (1) letter-spacing control with proportional word-spacing, (2) larger maximum text size (up to about 24 px/18 pt), (3) avoid italics, (4) a warm cream/peach light theme as an option or default test, (5) adjustable bubble width (line length), and (6) keep emoji as supports with a words-only opt-out. Myths or unproven: "dyslexia fonts" (OpenDyslexic, Dyslexie), Lexend/Atkinson as proven dyslexia aids, coloured overlays/Irlen as a treatment, Bionic Reading, dark mode as a dyslexia aid.

### Inferences
- Priority order for Lexi (by evidence strength x cost): (1) letter + word spacing setting; (2) larger top size; (3) italic ban in templates; (4) cream/peach theme and avoid cool blue/blue-grey as default surfaces; (5) bubble-width option; (6) keep emoji with words-only toggle; (7) leave fonts as-is (Verdana default is fine) without claiming proven benefits for Lexend/Atkinson.
- Things not worth building: overlay-as-therapy features, Bionic Reading mode, "dyslexia font" marketing claims, syllable colouring.
- Language to avoid in the app: "clinically proven", "reduces dyslexia symptoms". Safer: "many readers find ... easier; adjust to what suits you."
- Mood-journaling context may differ from the reading-passage tasks studied: journaling involves short, self-paced, emotionally salient text (inference); none of the cited studies tested chat/journaling UIs.

### Gaps
- Most dyslexia typography research is on children, in Spanish/Italian/Dutch/French, on desktop or paper, and measures speed rather than comprehension; very little on teens/adults on phones or on chat formats.
- Search constraints: Google Scholar/ACM DL not reachable; several PMC and publisher pages blocked by captcha/403; many details taken from PubMed abstracts, author-hosted PDFs, and secondary summaries as flagged. Anything flagged "(secondary)" should be checked before it is quoted externally. Studies published in 2026 may be missing.
- No evidence found on glare/brightness settings (e.g., auto-dimming) specific to dyslexia, on font weight, or on animation/typing-indicator effects in chat.
